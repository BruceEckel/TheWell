The Well
Act III · Chapter 4

The Death of Despair

The bond, when it came, came through the tune, which Kit would spend years deciding whether to forgive.

It was a January night in the print works, the two of them last as they now often were, collating the winter session's logs, and Kit, tired past his own perimeter defenses, hummed. Not performed; leaked: six notes up over the sorting, four down, the tail going out the floor as he squared the pages, and he was reaching for the stapler when he registered the quality of the silence behind him and turned to find Roque sitting absolutely still with his eyes shut and his face undone.

Not the teaching face. Not any face from the syllabus. The face of a man in a waiting room hearing his name pronounced correctly after a lifetime of clerks getting it wrong.

"Don't stop," Roque said.

Kit, unnerved, took it again from the top, felt like a fool, stopped feeling like a fool somewhere in the middle, and finished it properly, tail and all, in the cold room among the stacked chairs. The silence afterward went on a long time.

"My whole life," Roque said, eventually, eyes still shut, in a voice Kit had never heard him use on any stage, "there has been a sound just under the sill of hearing. Since I was a boy. Like a tune in the next house: you can't catch it, you can't prove it, you can only notice that every actual sound is not it." He opened his eyes. "That's the nearest anyone has ever hummed to it. Where is it from?"

"My mother's bakery," Kit said, which was true, and was nothing, and they both knew it, and from that night the teacher kept him late on different business, and the last winter began.

What Roque said in those late nights he said the way a man pays out rope, testing the belay, a length at a time. He was fifty-nine. He spoke of the Practicum's success as a man speaks of a spreading garden he suspects of being knotweed. And on the last night of February, with the canal ticking under the windows, he paid out the whole rope.

"I'll tell you the thing I deny at seminars," he said. "You've earned it by not asking. Thirty years I've taught that the house is finished and empty. No one home; nothing to fix; the watching is the whole of it. And I have gone in, Kit. Nightly, for thirty years, with the lamp of everything I know, to rest in the empty house the way I promise them rest. And it is not empty." He said it without drama, a surveyor reporting. "There is someone home. Down at the bottom of me, on some stair of me, there is someone standing, and he is waiting, and he has been waiting the whole of my life. Not for death; I know the shape of that wait, this isn't it. Waiting for something. Some errand, some arrival, some," the hand moved, precise, hopeless, "appointment. And here is the joke, brother, laugh when ready: I have spent my one life building the case that there is nothing to wait for. I've won the case. It's cited everywhere. So his waiting has no address, and a waiting without an address, do you know what the old books call that? They had a word. They called it despair, and they classed it a sin, which I used to find quaint, and I'll tell you what I think now: they classed it a sin because calling it an illness would have meant admitting there was no cure."

The canal ticked. And Kit, twenty-six years old, six years deep in the doctrine that had saved him, holding the confession of the man who had built it, did the worst thing he ever did, and did it out of love, with the best of everything he had.

He handed the teacher back his own cell key, polished.

"The someone on the stair," Kit said, gently, in the consult voice he had learned from its owner, "is the last piece of scaffolding. You taught me that. The builder's reflex, the ghost of the project self, the mind generating a waiter because it can't bear a finished house. You wrote it in the second book. It fooled you in the second person your whole career and now it's trying the first. There's no one waiting, and nothing coming, and that isn't the bad news, it's the good news, it's your good news, I got it from you." And more in that vein, fluent, tender, airtight, the whole beautiful apparatus deployed at the man it came from, and Roque listened with his eyes closed the way he had listened to the tune, and when Kit finished, the teacher was quiet, and then said, "Yes. That's the argument. It's a very good argument," in the tone of a man at a counter agreeing that the paperwork is certainly in order, and looked into his mug, and asked, as if changing the subject, whether Kit would be at the spring retreat, because he wanted, he said, to have the tune where he could reach it.

The spring retreat was the coastguard station, the headland, the shingle: the Practicum's oldest ground.

Roque swam at dawn, every dawn, all his life; it was in the books, it was in the brand; cold water as materials work, the body's alarm systems run deliberately and watched. The household rose to bells he'd made from old cylinder stock and did the morning watch while the founder was in the sea, and he came up the shingle blue and alight and made the kitchen laugh, and that was the retreat, that had always been the retreat.

On the third night he kept Kit up after the others, in the signal room, with the one lamp down low.

He was easy that night. That was the thing Kit would tell the inquiry, tell the household, tell himself, at first as evidence and then, for years, as indictment: he was easy, easier than Kit had ever seen him, the rope all paid out and nothing left to test. They did the logs. They talked about the autumn syllabus, about a paper Kit was writing, about bread, of all things, his mother's ovens, the proper knead. And at the end, packing up, Roque said, "Give me the tune. The whole of it. I want it where I can reach it."

Kit hummed it in the signal room: six up, four down, out the floor, and the tail went down into the boards of the old station and out, it seemed, into the black water beyond the glass, and Roque stood at the seaward window with his eyes shut, exactly where he had stood the autumn Kit had catalogued him as a finished house, and when it was done he said, quietly, "There it is," and then, with his back still turned, "Thank you, Kit. Go to bed, brother."

Roque's towel was found at the tide line in the morning, folded, his robe on top of it, weighted with a stone against the wind, which was what he did every dawn, which was what made it, the inquiry ruled, in the absence of any note, and in view of the currents that spring, and of the deceased's known and documented practice of cold-water swimming, an accident.

The sea returned him on the second day, four miles down the coast, and the word the household used at the gathering that night was released, and Kit stood at the back of the room with the word going through him like wire, because he alone had heard the rope pay out, he alone had been shown the someone on the stair, he alone had been handed, in a cold print works with the canal ticking, the plain confession, despair, named as despair, out of the old books, by the man himself, and had answered it, out of love, fluently, with the doctrine, had reasoned the last banister out of the man's hands and called it a gift, and then hummed him easy, hummed him easy, six up and four down and out the floor, the going gets easier, and gone to bed.

The beatification began before the funeral meats were cold, and it was the beatification, in the end, that broke the doctrine's spine for him, more than the death itself.

Within a month the household had a Roque it could use. The drowning grew a gloss: the founder's last materials work, the ultimate letting-go, ambiguity itself reframed as a teaching, he would have wanted the not-knowing, that was so him. The second book went to a memorial printing with a new foreword. There was talk, entirely serious, from people Kit had sat watches with for six years, people trained by the man himself to say only what was observably so, of keeping the signal-room lamp lit on retreat, permanently, as a remembrance: a lamp, kept burning, in a window above the water, for someone expected, and Kit listened to them design it, this shrine, this exact shrine, in the exact grammar of the thing the whole Practicum existed to refuse, and understood, with a clarity that arrived like the cold sea itself, what he was watching. He had seen it before. He had grown up over it. Grief in at the arch; doctrine out at the top; the man himself refined away into a face on the product. The lane had its incense timer, and the print works would have its lamp, and there was no place on the whole map, none, not the dome and not its opposite, that the machinery did not reach. They were building the reliquary by nightfall. All that ever varied was the font.

He resigned from the household in an email of four lines that he wrote eleven times, and packed his mainland life into two bags in an afternoon, eight years gone into boxes for the charity shop, and at the memorial, which he attended because love is not tidy, he stood at the back by the doors and found himself doing a thing he had not done since childhood, a thing from a shelved country: looking at hands. The household's hands, folded and correct. The publishers' hands. The hands of the woman with the cropped grey hair, who had fetched him from a foyer eight years ago, shredding a tissue with terrible slowness. He had begun, too late for one of them, to look at hands.

What he could not know, standing at the back in his coat with the boat ticket in his pocket, and what no one on the mainland knew or would have had a shelf for, was already true and already elsewhere: that somewhere, in a town of no significance, in the weather of an ordinary spring, a child had been born who would not sleep unless the window was open, as if listening for a sound from the next house; and that the searcher who might have found him in forty years, the only one alive with the method and the patience, was an old man across the water in a workshop full of clocks, who was running, now, by every calendar that mattered, out of time to search.

The boat left at six. Kit stood at the stern rail as the mainland went grey behind its own weather, and ahead, an hour out, the dome came up over the water the way it had come up for everyone forever, coin-colored, patient, keeping, and he watched it grow with two bags at his feet and nothing left in either world, going home the way the broken go home, which is to say not knowing why, hands in his pockets, gripping their nothing, counting.
