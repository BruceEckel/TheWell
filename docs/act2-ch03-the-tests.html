The Well
Act II · Chapter 3

The Tests

The rules were drawn up at the second Thursday, and Dara wrote them out by hand while Wren watched with the expression of a man at a christening.

Everything in writing. Everything dated. Claims sealed in envelopes before checking, checks made by Dara alone, no hints, no course corrections, no I meant to say. Wren was not to volunteer information about Dara's past, present, or family; he was to make claims about things findable in the world, and then he was to sit there and be wrong.

"Nobody has ever done this properly before," Wren said, signing the rules in a careful old hand. "Four hundred years, give or take, and you're the first one to bring stationery. I could weep."

"Four hundred years of what?"

"Of this conversation," Wren said. "Seal that too."

The first envelope held the dossier. Wren dictated it without pauses, like a man reading off an inner document worn soft from handling: that Dara had been, the last time, a man named Corin Vale, a clinical psychologist in the northern city of Aldermoor, dead seventy years now; that he had been censured, publicly, it made the papers, for a method built on the conviction that comfort was a drug and pain was a language; that he had died in a hospice with one visitor; that he had stood in doorways, held the card fan for a dying grandmother, wept once at a stranger's kindness and diagnosed himself out of it by morning. Cause of censure findable. Judgment published. Phrase to look for, Wren said, closing his eyes to read the inner page more carefully: iatrogenic comfort. His phrase. They took it off him and never gave it back.

Aldermoor's newspaper had digitized its archive back through the century, and Dara sat in the station's back office after a shift with the terminal and found it in eleven minutes: DISCIPLINE FOR CITY THERAPIST; "COMFORT CAN HARM," PANEL TOLD. A photograph, grainy, of a man on some institutional steps, sixty-odd, good coat, the face of a man concluding things. Dara sat and looked at the face for a long time. It did nothing for him; that was the strange part; it was nobody, an old stranger from an old paper, and he searched the face for himself the way you search a crowd for family and came away empty, and was surprised to find the emptiness a relief, and then surprised at the surprise.

But the judgment. He ordered the full text from the registry, eight days and a fee, and read it at his kitchen table, and there the vertigo got him, not in the face but in the voice. The panel has heard the respondent describe presence-oriented practice as, quote, a conspiracy to keep people from reading their own mail. The respondent maintains that his profession, quote, bills the crash victims for the crash.

Dara put the page down and got up and stood in the doorway.

His phrase. The barbecue phrase, his own, produced by his own mouth years ago, at a folding table, drunk, to laughter: billed the crash victims for the crash. Word for word in a dead man's disciplinary record from a city he'd never entered, printed before his mother was born. There was an explanation, of course, there is always an explanation: Wren had read the judgment decades ago, had hunted for a man who talked like that, had found the barbecue story secondhand from some station colleague. He wrote the explanation on the back of the envelope, as per his own rules, and dated it, and noted underneath, because the rules said record everything: W. was not at the barbecue. Nobody who was at the barbecue has ever left the peninsula. And the judgment sat on the table with its one phrase burning in it like a wire, and the explanation sat on the envelope, airtight, and for the first time in his life he caught himself, with both hands as it were, in the physical act of choosing the airtight over the true.

He did not tell Wren about the doorway, or the wire. He said: dossier partially verified, subject existed, correspondences suggestive, all channels compromised. Everything you know, you could have learned. Everything routes through you.

"Yes," Wren said, with the pride of a teacher whose student has found the flaw in the proof. "Everything routes through me. I'm the worst possible witness: I'm the only one who was there. So we need a claim that doesn't touch me." He folded his hands. "Your hum has a tail."

The claim, sealed that Thursday in envelope four, ran as follows, in Wren's careful hand:

The air D. hums is sung today as six notes rising, four falling. It was not always. Before the hymnal reforms, the air carried a third phrase: five notes falling past the home note, resolving low, what the old cantors called going out the floor. The reformed books cut it. It survives nowhere in use. It is preserved in the Weller Hymnal, printed before the reforms, of which the diocesan library at Sareth holds copies. D. has hummed the tail his whole life. D. has never heard it sung, played, or named.

He worked it like a case. Documentation first, claim second, archive last, in that order, so the archive could not contaminate the memory. The documentation was easier than it had any right to be: Pol had phone video from Sefton's retirement do, four years old, timestamped, Dara in the corner bandaging a hand someone had gashed on a keg ring and humming over the work, and there it was, plain as a recording could be: six up, four down, and then, absent-minded, automatic, while he tied off the dressing, five notes falling away underneath, out the floor. His mother, asked without context to sing what he'd hummed as a boy, produced the same fall and said, that's just how he did it, he put a little end on it, we thought he'd made it up. Made it up. It came through the radio. Two witnesses, one timestamp, all of it predating Wren by years, and Dara logged the lot and took the Friday boat.

He had not crossed since he was nine.

The strait behaved. Sareth came up over the water the way it had come up for everyone forever, and he stood on the deck with his coffee going cold and watched the dome do its slow trick of becoming actual, and the feeling in his chest he catalogued honestly, per the rules, as: dread, anticipation, and something with no clinical name that was neither, the feeling of an appointment. The Long Stair was dry that day. His feet found the groove at the third step, and he let them have it; he was thirty-four; there was nobody left to call him anything.

The diocesan library sat in the precinct's north range, past the temple square, and to reach it he had to cross the square, and he did that the way you cross exposed ground, at working pace, and the great doors stood open on his left with their gold-brown dark standing in them, and he did not look, and looked, once, the way you check a dangerous machine is still where you left it, and the dark looked back with its candles standing in it, and did nothing, this time, asked nothing, sent nothing through the seam, and he arrived at the library door with his heart going like a man post-lift and nothing on the monitor to show for it.

By the second pillar, a tour group was being told about the dome.

The library was church premises and he had expected to hate it and it would not be hated: it was a reading room, wood and good light and a radiator ticking, staffed by a small fierce woman who took his reader's slip and treated his request, the Weller Hymnal, pre-reform printing, as the most reasonable thing asked of her all week, which, she said, it was not, the competition being genealogists. The volume came up from the stacks smelling of cold stone. He sat at the long table with it and worked the index with clean careful hands, court discipline, chain of evidence, and found the air by its first line in the old orthography, and turned to the page.

There it was.

Six notes rising. Four falling. And beneath them, in the small square antique notation he had taught himself to read that fortnight from a library book, because the protocol said no experts, no interpreters, nothing between the evidence and the record: a third phrase. Five notes, falling past the home note, resolving low. Going out the floor. Note for note, the tail. The little end he'd put on it. In a book printed one hundred and forty years before his birth, cut from every hymnal since his great-grandmother's time, held in a locked stack across a strait he had crossed twice in thirty-four years, and humming, all of it, in the body of a man who had never heard it, since before he could talk.

Dara sat in the reading room with his hands flat on the table, one each side of the book, the way he'd been taught to leave a scene undisturbed, and the radiator ticked, and the gooseflesh went over him from the neck down, and he did what the protocol required: photographed the page, logged the shelf mark, thanked the librarian, walked out through the precinct at working pace, past the square, past the open doors and their patient dark, down the groove of the Stair, onto the boat, and only there, at the stern rail, with the city folding itself back into a thumbnail behind him, did he permit the finding.

The tail was in him. It had come from somewhere. There was no route. He had checked every route, and he was good, checking routes was the profession, and there was no route, and the explanation stood on the landing of him where it had stood all month, patient, lamp lit, and this time he stood in the doorway of it, which is not the same as going through, and looked in for a long moment at the lit room of it, and what he felt was not what he had braced for. Not collapse. Not the church, smiling, holding out its arms. Just size. The world with a floor taken out of it, going down and down, and him at the stern rail of the evening boat, a sound man, breathing, holding the rail, riding on top of it.

"Say it," Wren said, Thursday, gently, and Dara, who had faced the kitchen window to have his back to the room, said it.

"Something carries." His own voice, level, the handover voice. "I don't know what. I don't know how. But it isn't fraud and it isn't chance and it isn't the radio. The tail was in me before I could learn it, and the tail is real, and something carries." He turned around. "And I want you to understand what I am not saying. I am not saying souls. I am not saying what my mother lights the candle for. If you tell me next that it's all true, the whole shelf, the fire and the ore and the mercy of the burn, I'd rather have the fraud. I'll take the fraud with relief."

"It isn't the shelf," Wren said.

"Then what is it?"

The old man was quiet a moment, and in the quiet he looked, for once, all of his age, whatever that was. "You know how you go along the harbor road," he said, "and the strait's doing something impossible with the light, silver up the whole channel, and for a second it looks organized. Meant. And your lot say, it's not meant, it's optics, it's angles and water, and your mother's lot say, it's meant. And the true answer, which neither lot can stand, is that it's both, because somebody built the optics." He turned his mug the quarter turn. "There's a top of the well, Dara. It's not a heaven. It's a built place. It has rooms and power and things that hum in racks, and people, or what's left of some, and you did not condense out of nothing thirty-four years ago in Lorne, you came down, from up the well, on purpose, the way a man climbs down a rope he tied himself. That's as far as I'll go tonight, because the next part isn't information, it's freight, and you're at the end of a long shift of believing things."

And Dara stood with his back against his own counter, in his station of a flat, in the small kitchen of his one life, with the window over his shoulder keeping its eye on the thumbnail across the water, and heard himself begin, helplessly, to laugh. The bark first, the station's impression of him, one note, and then past it, into the real thing, minutes of it, leaning on the counter, wiping his eyes, laughing the way other men wept, and Wren sat and watched him with the patience of the very old and waited to be told.

"It's plumbing," Dara managed. "The afterlife. My gran's whole shelf. Forty generations on their knees in the wet, and it's," he gestured, helpless, at everything, "it's infrastructure. Somebody built it. It's got a maintenance schedule. Oh, she'd have hated you," and that sobered him half a degree, some tide of his grandmother going through, and he stood breathing, wrung out, lighter than he had been in a month, in a year, possibly in a life. Because if it was built, it wasn't holy. And if it wasn't holy, then the thing that had reached into him in that nave at nine years old had a mechanism, a schematic, a manual somewhere in some drawer, and a thing with a manual can be understood, and a thing that can be understood cannot own you.

That was the ledger he ran, standing in his kitchen, and it balanced, and he was, for exactly as long as it held, a free man.

"Provisionally," he said, pointing at Wren with the mug. "Everything provisionally."

"Provisionally," Wren agreed, with the face of a man who has been given, after four hundred years, the whole word he came for, and is far too old to spoil it by saying so.

That night, cleaning down the kitchen, Dara caught himself humming, and did not stop. He took it through the six and down the four and let it go on, deliberate for the first time in his life, out the floor, all five falling notes of the tail, to the end, and stood a moment in the quiet after it, listening, like a man who has signed something and is waiting to see how it feels.
