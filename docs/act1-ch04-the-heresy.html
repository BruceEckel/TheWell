The Well
Act I · Chapter 4

The Heresy

The plate on the door said DR. C. VALE, and under it there had been a second plate belonging to a colleague who had moved on, and the screw holes were still there nine years on, two small dark mouths, and the building manager kept promising to fill them. Corin had stopped reporting it. He had come to regard the holes as accurate.

By forty-one he had the reputation, and the reputation did his advertising. Colleagues sent him the stuck ones. They did not say stuck in the referral letters, they said treatment-resistant, they said has not responded to a supportive approach, and what they meant was: we have held this person's hand for four years and the hand-holding has become the illness. Then they sent the file to Corin the way you send a coat to the one cleaner who uses the harsh stuff, and they disowned the method at dinner parties, and they kept sending.

Harmon came in March, referred by a practice that had run out of upholstery. Fifty-five, retired at fifty on a good package, house paid off, marriage amiable, health sound, and dying. He'd done the courses. He'd done the mindfulness, the walking group, the medication, the second opinion, the third. He sat down in Corin's office, looked out the window, and said, "I want to want something. They keep telling me I'm doing well."

"You're not doing well," Corin said. "You're comfortable. Those are different animals."

Harmon looked at him for the first time.

Corin took him on. The treatment plan would have read, to the profession, like a workhouse sentence: cold mornings, manual labor at the city farm two days a week, a commitment to his brother-in-law's roofing crew that Harmon was not permitted to buy his way out of, and a standing rule, the only one Corin enforced with real severity, that nothing difficult could be delegated. Book your own appointments. Carry your own bags. Dig.

In the eighth week Harmon cried in session, for the first time in nine years, about his daughter, about something his daughter had said when she was six that he had not thought about since, and the crying came up out of him in dry hard pieces like brick out of a wall. In the sixteenth week he came in sunburned with a bandaged thumb and talked for the whole hour about a wall he was rebuilding, a real wall, stone, and did not once look out the window. At the end of it he wrote Corin a letter, which Corin kept, and the letter said, in the middle of it: Everyone else kept adjusting my pillows. I was disappearing into the pillows, and they called the disappearing peace, and you were the only one rude enough to say so.

That was the method working. It did work: the files were real, the recoveries were real, the stuck ones came to him fused shut, and a percentage of them, a good percentage, left with the hinges moving. He was not a crank. He was a clinician with results, a folder of grateful letters, and a theory, and the theory was the whole of him organized into a paragraph: that pain was information, that the profession had become a conspiracy to keep people from ever reading their own mail, that a therapy whose first commandment was you should not have to feel this was not a treatment but a tourniquet left on until the limb goes gray.

He believed it the way you believe a thing you have concluded. It had the feel of a conclusion. It had references. If anyone had told him that the conviction had come into the world before the evidence had, arriving with him like a birthmark, waiting years in the dark of him for arguments to grow around it the way a wall waits for ivy, he would have asked, with genuine professional interest, when they had stopped taking their medication.

Verey was thirty-four, and she came in June, two years after her husband's accident, referred with complicated grief, and Corin misread her on the first day and did not learn of it for five months.

The misreading was small. It would not have survived a silence, if he had permitted one. She presented composed and articulate and slightly ironic, the exact grain of a stuck one, and she said the thing the stuck ones said, they keep telling me to be gentle with myself, with the eye-roll, and Corin heard a woman upholstered half to death by two years of validation and made the plan: the storage unit she paid for monthly and had never once entered, where his clothes were, his tools, the wedding boxes. Open it. Not eventually. Thursday. He booked the session to follow it directly.

She opened the unit on a Thursday in November, alone, as assigned, and what Corin had taken for upholstery had been load-bearing, and it came down all at once.

She did not come to the follow-up. Her sister's first letter to the board was four pages; the attachments ran longer. Ms. Verey had stopped eating reliably; had been seen at the crisis unit twice; was living now at the sister's, sleeping in her niece's bunk bed, thirty-four years old. Page three had the sentence the board would read aloud twice: My sister went into that room composed, because composed was the only thing she had left that worked, and Dr. Vale confiscated it.

The hearing was in February, in a conference room with a long table and eleven chairs, and Corin counted the chairs and hated the number with an old private hatred he could not source.

They were not cruel. That was the worst of it; he had prepared for cruelty. The chair of the panel was a tidy man named Ostrand with reading glasses and a tremor in his left hand that he managed by keeping the hand flat on the table, and the panel had read everything, the Harmon letter too, the outcomes data too. They had done him that justice. They talked about unmodulated exposure and about the absence of a staged protocol and about consent that had been obtained but not, in the panel's view, informed, because the client had not been told the method was a departure. And then Ostrand took off the reading glasses and said the thing the profession actually meant, underneath the citations.

"Dr. Vale. Your position, as I understand your written statement, is that our field has made an idol of comfort, and that suffering, correctly administered, is the active ingredient. I want to be sure I have it fairly."

"Not administered," Corin said. "Permitted. The suffering is already in the building. The field's entire project is to keep it from getting into the room. I let it into the room, with a schedule and an exit, and the outcomes are in front of you."

"They are," Ostrand said. "Both kinds." He put the glasses back on and looked at his notes, and then over them. "You put it very well. You always put it very well; the statement was a pleasure to read, which is not something I say often at this table." A pause. "You know, my grandmother could have written its last paragraph. Hold still in the burning. You sound, Doctor, and I mean this as an observation and not a discourtesy, like a man of faith."

Two chairs down, somebody's pen stopped.

"The difference," Corin said, when he could, and he heard that his voice had gone quiet and could not fix it, "is that I lower the flame. That is the entire difference. That is the difference the whole of my protocol exists to make. They tell the burning to mean something. I tell it when to stop."

"Ms. Verey's sister," Ostrand said, not unkindly, "would ask, I think, whether you know how."

The finding was formal censure, published; conditions on practice, two years, high-intensity methods suspended; supervision monthly; and a mandated course of personal therapy, the third such recommendation of his career and the first with teeth. While it was read he sat with his hands flat on the table, both of them, like Ostrand's one, and from where he sat he could see through the conference room door, which the clerk had left open an inch onto the corridor, one inch of ordinary corridor, coats going by, and for the length of the reading he rested his eyes on the gap the way other men at such moments look at photographs of their families.

The mandated therapist was called Feld, an old man, in an office with plants, who had been retired twice and kept unretiring, and who was, everyone agreed, wasted on disciplinary referrals, and who took them anyway, on purpose, for reasons he was not known to have explained.

Corin went the way you go to the dentist, calendared, punctual, gloved. He gave clean history in efficient blocks. He flagged his own defenses as he deployed them, which he was aware was itself a defense, and flagged that too, meta all the way down, and Feld sat in his cardigan among the plants and let all of it come and did not reach for any of it.

In the fourth session Feld asked what he did when something hurt.

"I work," Corin said.

Feld nodded slowly, as if this were a full answer deserving of respect, and then did nothing. Said nothing. Sat. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below in the street a delivery van beeped itself backward into a space, all the way in, and finished, and drove off, and the room went on holding just the two of them and the question's afterimage, and Corin sat inside the lengthening quiet and felt it become enormous, felt it take on the pressure of deep water, and understood, with the special nausea of a man recognizing a technique from the wrong side of it, that this was the silence. The one he had never once permitted. Eleven observed sessions, the report had said. He had counted silences a failure of pace. From in here the silence was not a failure of anything. It was a room with nothing in it but you and the mail you had not read, and the old man was simply declining, with perfect courtesy, to be a distraction from it, and every second it went on, something was rising in Corin like water in a lock, and what rose was not memory and was not grief, nothing so organized. It was just pressure, tidal, unitemized, the whole unopened storage of him leaning on the one door, and he looked at the plants, and the door held.

"We're past time," Corin said, which was true by ninety seconds.

"So we are," Feld said, and wrote nothing down, and at the door, holding it open, added, in the tone of a man commenting on the weather, "You defend your clients from their pain much less well than you defend yourself from yours. For what it's worth, it was the second thing I noticed."

Corin attended every mandated session, and was compliant, and completed the course, and did not go back, and the file said engaged appropriately, because Feld was fair and the phrase was technically supportable, and years later the silence would still sometimes surface in him, unscheduled, the plants, the radiator, the water in the lock, filed and refusing to stay filed, in the drawer with the other things.

The drawer was real, was the thing. Bottom left of the desk that had been his father's. The rebuttal to Maro was in it, sixteen airtight pages, never shown to anyone. Elin's last note about the forwarding address was in it. And the evening the censure was published, he sat in the office with the two dark screw holes in the door and did the accounts, and could not make the page balance, for the first time since the method existed, because Harmon's letter said the fire had saved him and the sister's letter said the fire had taken what her sister had left, and both were true, and both were his, and a ledger is not built for a man who is right and wrong in the same column at once. He read them each twice. Then he put them in the drawer together, the letter and the letter, face to face in the dark, where they could argue it out between themselves for as long as they needed, which was, as it turned out, the rest of his life.

He kept practicing, inside the conditions. He was, the supervisor wrote monthly, cooperative and reflective, which he read in the car park and once, only once, laughed at, a short bark, alone, the maddest sound he had ever heard himself make, and there was no one anywhere near the car to tell him whose laugh it was.
