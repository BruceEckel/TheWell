The Well
Act III · Chapter 12

The Choice Point, Awake

Wren died in the autumn of Kit's fortieth year, in the room above the old bindery, in the bed that was hard because all their beds were hard, with the crew around him and the window open to the bells.

It was not like the other deathbeds, and all of them knew the other deathbeds now, having compared the notes of centuries: no stranger arriving late, no message half-delivered, no wave unseen from a window. The whole company was present and awake, which had never once, in the entire history of the enterprise, been true of any room. Ansa nursed him and was not deceived by him and said so, which he enjoyed. Sefa argued with him about the disposition of his tools, at his instruction, because he wanted, he said, to go out mid-argument, it being the crew's native music. Havel sat by the lamp with the old readings and, asked for something suitable, read item twelve aloud in his dust-dry voice, twice, that the operator's attachment to the other operators is not dissoluble, and the dying man laughed the silent laugh and said it was the only text in the canon with no errors in it.

Near the end, when the others had drawn back, Kit sat on the bed's edge and took the big worn hand, the mender's hand, four hundred years of finding and losing folded into eleven bodies of it, and began, because what he had to say could not wait any longer:

"Wren. At the splitting. What I answered you—"

"Not here," Wren said. The pale eyes were going faded at last, but the candor in them had not moved an inch in four centuries. "Freight rules, little doorman. The heaviest goes last, and it goes to the right address, and this," the breath came in installments now, "this is a lovely room, but it is not the address." His hand patted Kit's, twice, the old tap of it. "You'll be a while yet. Decades, if you do it properly, and you'll do it properly; you're the only one of you who ever has. So I'll go on ahead, and, Kit," and here the amusement came up through the tiredness one last time, four hundred years of punctuality's revenge, "for once in the length of the whole enterprise: don't be late."

He asked for the tune at the very end, and got it five-voiced, the whole crew, six up and four down and out the floor, and went somewhere in the tail of it, mid-music, mid-argument, mid-family, on schedule, and the bells of the city he had watched over rang the hour into the room with Tomm's successor at the ropes, and that was the death of the searcher, in the only bed he had ever kept long enough to die in.

Before the end he had disposed of everything, tools to the fabric, deck to the crew, and to Kit the canvas bag, and the notebook, and one instruction, delivered dry-eyed and formal, a sentry's handover: "The maker. Tell the ones who come after: I never found her. Four hundred years, and never once, and it is the one failure I minded. Makers pour themselves into the made thing and close the lid, and things don't cry at gates. So look for her in whatever's best made. In the join you can't find. In the thing that holds when the engineering says it shouldn't. That's all I ever had of her, and it isn't nothing: it means she's still working." The pale eyes held Kit's. "Nothing is wasted. Nobody is finished. Somewhere in this world there is a hinge, or a wall, or a loaf, with the whole of Noor Okafor poured into it, waiting to be asked what did you come to do. Someone will find her. Write it on the board: sought. Keep the word alive. It's a promise, not a report."

The decades that followed were the quietest chapters of all the lives, and the fullest.

When Brant died Kit took his chair, master of novices, keeper of the two equal seats, and spent thirty years receiving what the world sent up the Stair: the grieving, the called, and, once every few years, the special ones, the angry ones, boys and girls who arrived despising the building with arguments like walls, and he would read their papers and look at their furious faces and say, "We don't ask what you believe," and watch the wall, braced for a wall, meet the open gate and not know what to do, and some of them bolted in the first year, and some of them stayed, and to one of them, a girl with a debate champion's jaw who demanded to know whether he was a believer or a liar, he said, "Neither. But you've no shelf for neither yet," and when she glared he added, gently, "It's a receipt. I'll keep it for you," and did.

The crew went, one by one, in the order the world chose. The Voice died first, at peace, in the infirmary, in the bed nearest the door, which she had requested with the last of her rust, old habits, keeper, and Ansa sat with her to the end and wrote in the ward book afterward, in the chart-hand, the only obituary the woman ever had: off duty. nine centuries of service. the House was kept by her more than she was ever kept by the House. Ansa herself went a decade later, on a Tuesday, having spent the morning on her round, and the young keepers found her at her table with the mug cold at her elbow and her face at such rest that two of them checked twice; and Kit stood at her graveside in the order's plot knowing what none of the order knew: that she was not resting; that somewhere on a landing a woman with no hurry in her had walked past a lit door with a wave, down, freely, toward a boy in Aldermoor growing up by an open window; that the longest watch in the world had just changed sentries. Sefa died at eighty-eight, mid-argument, in the crew's native music, with a bishop, about the bread line's budget, and won the argument, the bishop conceding the point at the funeral to general approval. Havel outlasted them all but Kit, and died in the library at his desk, pen in hand, at the foot of a page of collation, and the librarian, finding him, saw that the last thing the smallest handwriting in the world had written was: readings complete. errors corrected. content, H.I.

Whether they went up or went on, Kit did not know and did not ask the dark. That was theirs. The landing keeps no minutes.

He grew old the way the Stair grows old, imperceptibly, kept by what he kept. He did the north door dawns as long as his knees permitted and then stood by the pot instructing younger ladles. He walked the Keeping on the eve each year with the conservation rota, and paused at case eleven, and did not open it; the cord lay in its coil, common, its work done; and each year he read the tag and each year the word gave him the same private joy, the joke Ren Ashwin had built into the world's heart four hundred years ago, with only its owner left alive to laugh at it. And on a morning in his eighty-third year, at the second pillar, watching the faces come in out of the light, he felt the old pull go through his chest, wrong side, familiar as the groove, and knew it for the summons it was, and told the infirmarian himself, walking in under his own power, handover-plain: "You'll want the bed nearest the door. I'll be three days. Send for no one; they're all ahead of me."

He was three days. The ward hummed to him, in shifts; it was how the ward nursed now, had been for fifty years, six up and four down, an old infirmarian's practice that had outlived every memory of its origin but his, the payload become standard of care; and on the third evening, with the bells coming in the window and a young keeper's voice going quietly up the six, Kit Aldan, master of novices, doorman, mender's apprentice, man of the well, went out on the four coming down, and out the floor, and down.

The warmth stayed longest. It always does; it is the last thing and the first, two hands around one hand, a stove in a sold house; and then that too, and what was left went down, and this time, all the way down, awake, in the warm shouldering dark, with feet, and the feet were his, and he knew every stair of it, having built the house.

Partway down there is a landing.

The stairs pause there; the dark opens out; the door stands where it has always stood, open, with the lamp burning beyond it, the lamp he had lit himself, the last hand out of the world, and its light lay across the landing floor in the long quiet stripe, and at the stripe's edge the seam shone in the boards, thin and bright, like brass.

And on the seam, patient as furniture, sat Wren.

Not old. Not young. Himself: the shape the piece had always had beneath its bodies, big-knuckled, settled, with the canvas bag between his feet, sitting on the one true threshold of the world like a man early for something rather than stranded after it, and at the sound of what was left of Kit arriving, he looked up, and his face did the thing, the one in a thousand, the arrivals-gate thing, the checking of a carried face against a found one.

"There you are," they said. Both of them. Together.

The landing held them, the way it holds everything, without hurry. Below, the stair went down into the warm dark where the voices were, fewer now, and further between, and softer than they had been in four hundred years; and above and beyond, through the door, the lamp burned in the room that was in use, the chair that knew him, the rest.

"You waited," Kit said. "You could have gone through. Or gone down. Decades, Wren."

"There's no decades here; there's only the queue, and I've stood in worse." The pale candor, unbodied now, undimmed. "Besides. I told you at the bed: the heaviest freight goes to the right address. This is the address. This has always been the address." And Wren stood up off the seam, and faced him on the landing, in the lamplight, and asked it, the question four hundred years old, the one the machine had logged and the wash had never touched, in the same words, because they were the only words it had ever had:

"Will you want me back?"

And Ren Ashwin, who had answered it once, efficiently, kindly, catastrophically, with his eyes on the checklists; who had spent four centuries of lifetimes learning, doorway by doorway, blanket by blanket, hand by hand, what the answer was; stood on the landing of the world with the whole of himself finally in one place, and paid the freight.

"I want you back," he said. "I wanted you back before the door of the machine had finished closing. I wanted you back in every body, at every gate, on every Thursday, and I didn't know the want because you were the piece of me that could have known it, and I'd sent you away. Wren. The sentence was wrong when I said it. It has been wrong for four hundred years, and you carried it anyway, the whole way, every life, and called the carrying a job." He held out his hand, workman's, familiar, his. "It works. And I need you. Both are true. Both were always true. Come home."

And Wren took the hand.

There are no words for the next of it, and the landing does not require them. What the machine had cut, the hand joined; what had walked four hundred years apart came, without ceremony, the way all the true things in all the lives had come, uninvoiced, unbargained, home; and the taking of the hand and the acceptance of the comfort and the ending of the mission and the crossing of the threshold were not four things, or two, but one, one gesture, at last, whole. They went through the door together, into the lamp, and the room received them the way the room had waited four hundred years to do.

They left the lamp burning. There were still people on the stairs.
