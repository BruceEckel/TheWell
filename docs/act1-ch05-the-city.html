Act I · Chapter 5

The City

The congress was called Loss and Resilience: Integrative Directions, and it was held that year in Sareth, and Corin registered within an hour of the email going out, which he noticed himself doing and explained to himself immediately. The explanation had three points. It was the only national meeting where his censure had become citable rather than disqualifying, there being a session this year on iatrogenic comfort, his phrase, used without attribution in the program, which alone justified attendance. The train was direct. And a man who worked as much as he did was owed, by any accounting, four days somewhere that was not his office.

Three points. He was fifty. He had learned nothing about airtight.

The train went up the coast the last hour, and he had a seawall in his head the whole way, some angle off a poster he must have absorbed decades ago, and when the city finally came around the headland it came exactly wrong: bigger, dirtier, hemmed with cranes and a container port the poster had cropped out, ferries crossing the harbor like beetles. And then the train leaned into the curve and the light did something and there it was inside the bigger dirtier thing, the old town stacked up its hill, pale roofs going up like dealt cards, and at the top the dome, which the guidebook called weathered bronze and which was, in fact, in that light, the exact color of a coin kept too long in a pocket, and Corin sat with his book open against his sternum and had, for the length of the curve, the feeling of arriving to check on something rather than to see it. The feeling of the key turning in a door you have never locked.

The feeling filed itself under long journey, low blood sugar. There was a dining car. He used it.

Sareth at street level was a pilgrim economy wearing a heritage economy wearing a port. The lower town was normal, banks and phone shops and men unloading fish. The old town began at an arch, and through the arch everything tilted upward and went narrow, and the shops changed species: candles in seven sizes, cup pins in tin and silver and gold plate, framed lines from the Gatherings in fourteen typefaces, and the pilgrims themselves, everywhere, instantly identifiable and not by dress. By walk. They walked like people carrying full cups. A big woman in hiking gear had a photograph pinned inside her open coat, facing in, where she could look down at it. A man Corin's age climbed on two canes, slowly, refusing the four-coin funicular that ran alongside, and his face as he climbed had the concentration of a man doing arithmetic he had paid to have done for him. All of it was catalogued by the professional apparatus in Corin's head, unmodulated exposure, sunk-cost devotion, the pain market. The apparatus ran hot the whole first day and produced its usual excellent unpublishable paper. And under the apparatus, quietly, the way a building's other pump runs under the one you can hear, something in him was counting the streets going up, and knew the count was wrong, and knew what the right count would be, and he was aware of this the way you are aware of a sound below hearing, which is to say he was not aware of it. He was only tired, and drank more coffee than usual, and slept in the hotel like a man dropped from a height.

The congress was the congress. He sat through iatrogenic comfort and heard his own decade-old argument come back to him wearing a young woman's voice and better statistics, and the field nodded along at what it had censured him for, adjusted now, softened, administrable, and he had expected to feel vindicated and instead felt like a man watching his coat walk past on someone else it fit.

The third afternoon was posters, which meant free.

He told himself he was going up for the architecture, and made it true by buying the architectural guide at the arch, a receipt for the ledger. The Cradle of Sareth, begun nine centuries ago, unfinished for four, the dome added late. The pilgrim route went up a stepped street called the Long Stair, ninety-odd steps in flights of irregular sevens and nines, and the guide had a paragraph about the irregularity, disputed, possibly structural, possibly doctrine. Corin climbed it in the flow of climbers. Nobody hurried. The stair had a grain to it, an accumulated smoothness down its center where the feet went and had gone for nine hundred years, a groove worn by nothing but arrival, and he found his own feet drifting into the groove and made them stop, and then stopped making them.

The Cradle did not appear gradually. That was the trick of the approach, the guide said. The builders had done it on purpose: the Stair turned a last blind corner between high walls and delivered you all at once into the temple square, into open sky and the whole pale front of the Cradle, the doors, the dome overhead like weather.

The square was full of ordinary afternoon. Pigeons, a school group being counted, a sweeper working the base of the steps with a broom too short for him. The pilgrim queue ran up the center of the square to the great doors on a route marked by nothing, no ropes, no signs, just nine hundred years of agreement, and it moved steadily, and the doors stood open, both of them, folded back against the outer wall, and past them was a depth of gold-brown dark with candlelight standing in it, and somewhere in that dark, said the guide, page forty, beyond the far screen, lay the Keeping, the oldest closed room in the country, opened to the laity four days a year, housing the founding relic and the accumulated votive holdings of nine centuries.

Corin stood at the edge of the square and read page forty, and his hands were shaking.

He observed this. He put it through the apparatus. Exertion: ninety-some steps, at fifty, after coffee. Hypoglycemia again, possibly: posters had eaten the lunch hour. The apparatus submitted its findings and the findings were rejected, quietly, by the body itself, which went on shaking, not grossly, nothing anyone would see, a fine tremor at ten past the hour on both hands, and with the tremor came a pressure he had felt once before in an old man's office full of plants and never since, water in the lock, a leaning of everything against one door, except that in Feld's office the leaning had come from inside him and here it came from in front of him, from across the square, out of the open gold-brown dark, as if the building were the patient and the building had been waiting nine hundred years for its appointment and had recognized, in the crowd, the one man in it who was signed in.

That was not a thought. He did not permit it as a thought. It went through anyway, once, whole, the way weather crosses a border without documents.

He did not join the queue. He stood at its edge, at the exact seam where the marked-by-nothing route began, feet on the seam, and the queue went past him at his shoulder, the woman with the inward photograph, the man with two canes arriving at last, breathing in fours, a girl carrying her grandmother's cup pin wrapped in a handkerchief to be blessed, each of them passing from the square's white light into the doorway's brown gold and going soft at the edges and then going in, and Corin's whole body stood at the seam like a compass needle held one degree off north with a finger, and the finger was his, and holding it there took everything the finger had.

It's a door, he told himself. It is a decorated door into a dark room that smells of wax, and past it another door, into a smaller darker room full of dead people's kept things, and there is nothing in either room that survives contact with a lux meter, and you are a clinician having a paroxysm of the disease you specialize in, at altitude, unfed. Go down and eat.

"You can have my place," a voice said. "I've stopped minding how long it takes."

The old woman had come out of the queue and was standing beside him. Small, in the good coat of a person for whom this was the year's event, offering, with one tilted hand, the gap she'd left. Her eyes went over him with a speed and completeness he knew professionally, took in the shaking, the seam, the guidebook held like a shield, all of it, one pass, no verdict.

"You've been standing longer than any of us," she said. Kindly. As information.

"I'm not going in," Corin said, and his voice came out with a calm he had not sent it out with, a laboratory calm, and the sentence sat between them in the square and finished meaning what it meant and kept going, meant more, kept meaning, and the woman nodded slowly, as though he had said something true about the weather in a language she only half knew.

"No," she agreed. "Not today." She patted his sleeve once, forgave the queue its progress in her absence, and went back to the gap, which had waited for her.

He walked down the Long Stair with his hands in his pockets. His hands stopped shaking on the fourth flight. He counted flights going down, which he had not done going up, and the count came out at eleven, and he stood at the bottom in the ordinary street and laughed the short bark once, at nothing he could have named, and a man selling candles looked up and then away, being in a trade where such sounds were stock.

The last evening he skipped the closing plenary and walked out along the seawall instead, which was, the ledger noted, free.

The evening did the thing the old poster had promised. That was the disorienting part. The city was better at being the poster than it had any right to be: mauve coming up out of the sea's edge, the stacked windows catching one by one as the sun went, lights climbing the hill in no order, and at the top the dome holding its coin-color longest and then quietly giving it up. Corin stood at the rail where the photographer must have stood, give or take twenty meters he could not stop trying to calculate, and watched a city he had no history with go through its evening as if he had watched it do this from this rail on other evenings, in the dream, in the poster years, on the bench with his schoolbag between his feet, and the rail beside him, where in the dream there had always been the someone he never turned to look at, held its length of cold galvanized nothing, and he caught himself glancing at the nothing, twice, three times, irritated, the way you check a platform for a colleague who is late, and made himself stop, and gripped the rail, and the metal was the exact cold of the aluminum strip of a laundromat threshold he had stood on once at two in the morning in another life of his life.

The overdue feeling did not lift. He had expected the trip to pay it. That was, he saw now, the actual invoice under the three points, under the congress and the direct train. He had come here the way you finally return the library book, and he stood on the seawall with the book still in his hands, unreturned, the fine still running, somewhere a room he had not entered, and he was fifty, and it was cold, and he went in and had the set dinner and took the morning train.

From the curve past the headland the old town assembled itself once behind the glass, pale roofs, the dome, one gull holding station off the seawall as if paid to. He had seen it. That was done, he told himself, in the concluding voice, the voice for ends of things, and the city went behind the headland, and the feeling in his chest, filed before it could be read, was not the feeling of a thing concluded. It was the feeling on the landing when the phone stops ringing below, and you stand there in the sudden quiet, certain of nothing except that it will ring again, and that you will be older, and that the stairs will be longer going down.
