Act II · Chapter 6

The Teacher

The transfer to Saltmead came up on the board in the ordinary way, an eighty-nine-year-old going back to the home after a hip, a bread-and-butter run that the roster gods would normally hand to the newest crew, and Dara took it, swapping for it with a casualness that deceived nobody at the station and was not meant to deceive Wren, who had said only, the Thursday before: she's at the place on the salt road now. Has been eleven years. Go and look, next time the job takes you; don't make a door.

Saltmead sat behind its pines where the marsh road gave up, a low brick place with good windows. They brought Mr. Ostler in through the rain on the trolley at nine at night, and she met them at the door.

He knew her before she spoke. Not in the way he was braced for, no shiver of old acquaintance, no face surfacing from a newspaper of the soul; he knew her the way the trade knows its own landmarks, instantly, by silhouette: the night manager. Fifties, cardiganed, reading glasses up in the grey of her hair, a mug of something long cold parked on the medicines trolley, and the specific weather of her, that stillness with the hum under it, a sheltered anchorage of a woman. The corridor behind her was dim and breathing. She stood in the lit doorway of it like the keeper of a small harbor.

"Mr. Ostler," she said, to the trolley, before any handover could begin, bending down into the old man's eyeline. "You've been in the wars again, my love. Your Sandra rang; she'll come at the weekend; the room's warm." And to the shape the old man made under the blanket at the sound of the name Sandra, some settling of grief and relief that Dara felt through the trolley handles, she said nothing, only laid the back of her fingers along his cheek for a moment, the way you check bread.

The handover was flawless. She knew the hip, the meds, the daughter's number, that Ostler took tea at four in the night when he thought no one minded, and would want telling that the tide was in, because he'd been a dredger and the tide mattered. She wrote nothing down. It was all simply in her, the whole house of them, forty-some residents deep, and Dara, who assessed competence for a living the way other men noticed weather, stood in the doorway of Saltmead and understood that he was looking at the best clinician he had ever seen, working for care-home wages at nine at night on the salt road, eleven years without leave.

"You're the one who hums," she said to him, at the door, as they left.

He stopped. "Sorry?"

"The crews talk," she said, mildly. "Everything reaches here eventually; we're the end of the road. They say there's one who hums, and the going gets easier." She looked at him a moment longer than the remark needed, reading glasses down now, and her eyes behind them were kind and utterly without rest, and something moved in the very back of them the way a fish moves under a boat, deep, unhurried, gone. "There's worse to be known for," she said. "Mind the step going out."

In the cab, rain on the windscreen, Dara sat without starting the engine.

"Well," Wren said, from the dark of the passenger seat, where he had ridden along in defiance of every regulation Dara had decided not to remember that night.

"She's not sad," Dara said, slowly, working it. "I went in expecting, I don't know. Chains. A weeping under things. There's nothing. There's a good woman doing brilliant work, and if you put her in front of any panel in the country they'd give her a medal, and she'd deflect it onto the staff, and mean it." He started the engine. "That's her, isn't it. The one who went down out of love."

"That's her."

"How long has she," and he had to stop and restate the question in units that would fit through his own mouth. "How many Saltmeads?"

"I stopped counting the buildings," Wren said. "They're always brick. There's always a corridor and a mug going cold." The old voice was gentle and terrible. "You asked me once what the trap feels like, and I dodged, freight rules. Here's the freight, you've seen her now, you can lift it: it feels like that. It feels like being needed. It doesn't grip, Dara. It doesn't have to. It just keeps the corridor dim and the doorbell working, and every night at nine somebody's Sandra rings, and love does the rest, forever." He looked out at the pines. "She said a thing to me once, forty years back, different building, same mug. A resident was dying slow and I asked her, testing, why she thought it took the time it took. And she said: he's working on something, this one. They're all working on something. You can't hurry it. She knows, you see. Her hands know the whole of it. It's only the top of her that's asleep, and the top of her is the kindest thing on this coast, and I have never once," the voice went thin, "in four hundred years, found the way to make the top of her want to be woken."

Dara drove them home through the rain, and the wipers kept their metronome, and neither of them hummed.

The teacher played the Corn Hall in October.

TOMAS VANE, the posters said, black on white, no image, which was itself a fluency in image: AGAINST CONSOLATION: AN EVENING. Sold out five weeks ahead; the touts outside were getting temple-festival prices; the crowd filing in was every crowd Dara had ever mistrusted at once, readers, nodders, women with the new book hugged like a hot brick, men in good knitwear composing their questions for the interval, and he sat down in row J beside Wren with the folded arms of a man attending a rival's demonstration, and by the twelfth minute the arms had come unfolded, and he never afterward located the moment it had happened.

Vane was sixty and looked it and used it. No slides, no music, one chair he never sat in. He came out, looked at them all for a slow moment, and began, conversationally, in the middle of things: "Your church tells you the fire does not hate the ore." Pause. "I've come to bring you better news and worse. There is no ore." And the hall went silent the way the nave had gone silent, the exact silence, Dara clocked it with a lurch, the settling of a cold room when the heat comes on.

And then for ninety minutes, without notes, he was superb. That was the fact of it, and Dara sat in row J and suffered it. He said the things Dara had said drunk at a barbecue, sober and better. He took the profession apart, the pillow-plumping industry, he called it, the sedation of the species, and the hall laughed, and Dara laughed, once, before he caught himself, the bark, and Wren's hand patted the air, easy. He told them comfort was the business model of every institution that had ever farmed a human herd, church, state, and clinic alike, and that pain was not a malfunction, pain was the only honest correspondent left, the one letter-writer who never flattered, and that everything they had been taught to do with suffering, soothe it, dose it, meaning-make it, was a way of not reading the mail. Dara heard his own coinages come off the stage like debts being read out. Iatrogenic comfort, Vane said, twice, giving it the little pause of a man setting a jewel he knows is borrowed, and got a murmur of appreciation for it, and somewhere in a drawer in a dead man's desk in Aldermoor, Dara thought, wildly, sixteen airtight pages stirred in their sleep.

But it was the end that took the arms off him. The last ten minutes Vane dropped the wit entirely, and the voice came down to the register of a man talking in a kitchen, and he said: I'll tell you what I actually think, since you've paid. I think you know. I think every person in this hall wakes at three in the morning inside a life that fits them like a rented coat, and lies there, certain, for one clean moment before the sedatives of daylight kick in, that they were meant to be doing something else. Something specific. Something they've forgotten. Most of you are living like you forgot what you came for. And every peddler of comfort in this country is selling you a way to sleep through that, and I am here, one night only, to tell you the three-in-the-morning feeling is the only true thing you own. Don't let anyone soothe it. It isn't a symptom, it's a summons, and no, I don't know to what, nobody knows to what, there is no to-what, that's the terrible joke, the summons is the destination. Stand in it anyway.

The hall came up like a tide. Around Dara they were on their feet, some of them crying, holding their books, and he found that he was standing too, that his hands were making the noise the other hands made, and that his chest was doing something anatomically familiar and contextually obscene, the swell, the lift, the nave-feeling, here, in the Corn Hall, at a ticketed event, wearing his own clothes.

Wren had not stood. Wren sat small in the standing crowd with his hands folded on the head of the stick, looking up at the stage with an expression Dara caught sideways and could not file: pride, and grief, and a kind of connoisseurship, the face of a man at the concert of a son who plays beautifully and has been playing the same wrong note for three hundred years.

He queued for the signing. He told himself it was fieldwork.

Up close, Vane was better and worse: better, because the attention he turned on each reader was real, a full second of actual seeing, no small currency at the end of a two-hour show; worse, because at the range of a table's width Dara ran the assessment without wanting to, the trade's old reflex, and got back something that stopped his breath for exactly one wiper-beat before the explanation arrived to cover it.

He knew the face. Not Vane's face; the face under it, the one that surfaced in the half second between one reader leaving and the next arriving, when the seeing switched off and nothing came on in its place. Dara had met that face professionally perhaps a dozen times. You met it on parapets. You met it, more often, an hour after the parapet, in the back of the rig, wrapped in the blanket, thanking you, being charming, asking after your shift, and the thing about that face was that it was excellent company, it was the most reasonable face in the world, and every medic on the peninsula knew what you did with it, which was: you did not leave it alone with itself, and you handed it over, flagged, named, watched.

Then the next reader stepped up, and the seeing switched on, warm as stage light, and Vane was signing Dara's copy, and the explanation covered everything the way explanations did: the man's on tour, forty dates, he's sixty, he's tired, you're diagnosing exhaustion at a book table, physician of the year. Vane wrote something and handed the book back and, for his one full second, looked at Dara.

"You didn't want to come tonight," Vane said. Not a question; the trick of the trade, and Dara knew the trick, and it worked anyway.

"No."

"And?"

"You're the only one of them who sounds awake," Dara said, which he had not planned to say, and Vane's face did something at of them, some minute tilt of the needle, a compass passed over buried iron, and for a heartbeat the room seemed to go quiet around the two of them the way rooms had gone quiet all Dara's life, and then Vane smiled the stage smile and said, "Nobody's awake, friend. There's just insomnia of better quality," and the line got its laugh from the queue behind, and the second was over, and outside in the drizzle Wren was waiting under the awning like the last member of some disbanded order.

They walked to the station in the wet. Dara held the book inside his jacket.

"Say it," he said, finally, to the old man's silence.

"Every word is nearly true," Wren said. "That's the whole file on him. Four centuries, and the file never changes: every word, nearly true, and the miss is always the same four words." The stick clicked on the cobbles, keeping its own count. "There is no ore. That's his cell, Dara, he built it himself and he tours it. Because if there's no ore, nothing's being made of you, and the fire's just fire, and the summons has no address, and a man can stand anything, anything at all, except that. So he stands on stages and hands out his cell key to full houses, and they cheer, and every hand he shakes is another night he doesn't have to be alone in it, and he is the loneliest man alive, and he's getting better at it every life, and that," the old voice came apart slightly, one dovetail joint of it, "that is the worst part."

Dara walked, and the rain came on harder, and the book sat against his ribs with the warmth a book has when it has been held by many hands, and he did not take it out of his jacket, and did not throw it away either, and days later, on nights, at three in the morning in the mess room, a colleague would look over at him and say, good book?, and he would realize he had read it twice.
