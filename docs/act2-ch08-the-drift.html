The Well
Act II · Chapter 8

The Drift

The years after the door clicked shut were, by every measure the world kept, his best.

He took the training post he had twice refused, because the alternative was Thursdays with nothing in them, and found he was good at it, which he had known, and that it was bearable, which he had not: a classroom of the almost-competent being made competent, checklists, scenarios, the transmissible part of the trade. He taught the blanket in three movements. He heard himself say, to a room of nineteen-year-olds with their pens going, the survivor doesn't need your feelings, they need your hands to know the order of operations, and the nineteen-year-olds wrote it down, and one of them, a serious girl in the second row, asked, "Is it true you used to hum?" and he said, "No," and the room, sensing weather, moved on.

He rose. Station officer, then the peninsula's clinical lead, then a title with regional in it that meant meetings on the mainland and a car allowance. The obituary, he sometimes thought, in the three a.m. accounting, was shaping up beautifully. Marek modernized response protocols across four districts. Marek reduced time-to-scene by an average of ninety seconds. Marek was respected by everyone and known to no one, his crews would have died for him and could not have named his favorite anything, and he went home to the flat above the chandlery, which he had never left, though the money said leave, because the money did not understand about the window.

He did not hum. The stopping had been involuntary at first, a string cut somewhere; later it was policy; later still it was simply true, the way a language stops being yours if no one speaks it back.

His mother died the third spring, in her sleep, a kindness he distrusted on principle, the machine being, he now knew, capable of kindness the way a press is capable of gentleness, calibrated, for a reason, somebody's reason. At the funeral, Ostrine, her wishes, he sat in the front row and mouthed nothing, jaw set, and the speaker said she was done with what she came to do, and Dara sat there knowing what the sentence meant now, knowing it literally, a wound getting its turn, the turn complete, and the knowing sat in him like swallowed glass, because if it was true it was unbearable and if it was unbearable it was, by the whole shape of his refusal, not permitted to be true.

Afterward he went to the house to sort her things and found the candle on its shelf, burned to a thumb's width, out.

He stood in front of it for a long time. Then he took a new candle from the drawer where she had always kept them, a lifetime's supply, bought steadily, in ones and twos, against exactly this, and he lit it from a match and set it in the holder, and he did not pray, and he did not stay, and he did not examine it. He drove out twice a week thereafter to the empty house he could not yet sell, and kept the candle lit, and if asked, though no one alive was left to ask, he would have said it was policy.

At three in the morning he read Vane.

He knew the book was a door he shouldn't stand in and he stood in it. Because Vane's four words were, he had come to see, the only exit anyone had ever offered him from the arithmetic. If there is no ore, nothing is being forged, and if nothing is being forged, the fire has no purpose, and a fire with no purpose has no engineers, and a fire with no engineers has no arsonists, and a man can be, once again, what Dara had been at thirty-four and thought himself unhappy: a fireman. Just a fireman. Blameless, useful, wet. He would lie in the dark with the book on his chest and feel the four words do their work, the guilt going numb along the limb the way a tourniquet numbs it, and some nights he let it, and lay there sedated by the only sedative he had ever accepted, and other nights he took the tourniquet off in disgust because he knew what tourniquets cost by the hour, and the pain came back on like a floodlit yard, and that was the drift, that was all of the drift, twenty years of a man alternating between a poison and its antidote and calling the alternation health.

He looked, in those years, increasingly like something he could not place. He caught it in mirrors at conferences, in the dead glass of the train window on the mainland runs: a kept man, kept well, silvering, sound, and behind the soundness a face he almost knew. He put it down to his father, whom he was coming to resemble. It was not his father. He had met the face at a book table once, in the half second between one reader and the next, and had explained it away that time too, being, in every life, so far, a man who could explain anything.

The call came on a Tuesday in his forty-sixth year, and he took it himself because the station was stretched, flu season, and he still kept his hand in, the clinical lead who rode along, it was in the newsletters.

Lodging house on Tide Row, the dispatcher said. Male, eighties, collapse, landlady on scene.

He knew at the door. Not from the address, though later he would understand he should have; from the smell of the stairwell, tea and coal and old coats, the smell of a certain kind of kept life, and then the landlady's face at the top saying quick, he's asking, and the room, when he came into it, was a station of a room: bed hard, kettle fast, boots by the door, one window, and the window faced the strait, and under the window in a chair sat the canvas bag, handles up, packed, like a thing that had been packed for years, and in the bed, small now, gone to paper, with the winter light on him, was Wren.

"There you are," Wren said.

The trade took over, because the trade was what he had; hands doing the assessment while the world stood in the doorway of him and did not come in. It was the heart, and it was finished with its work, anyone in the trade could see it, the numbers were a courtesy. Wren watched him work with the old amusement, the boat-builder watching the boy check the boat, and when Dara reached for the radio to call it in, escalate, the whole apparatus, the old hand came up off the blanket and rested on his wrist, no weight at all, a leaf.

"You're allowed to put me in the ambulance," Wren said. "It doesn't count as carrying." The breath came in short paid installments now. "But it won't be needed. I know this road. I've a schedule to keep, and for once, Dara, for the first time in the length of the enterprise, I am not late." The pale eyes were entirely, terribly sane, and the amusement in them now was the deep kind, the kind at the bottom of the well of a person. "Six years on Tide Row. She'll tell you, the good woman, she thinks I didn't know she noticed: I always asked which crew had nights. I wasn't going to do this twice." A pause for breath, or for aim. "You shut the door before the wave came down. I'd like it on the record, while there's a record: I waved anyway. Every Thursday. From the corner. You just weren't at the window."

"Wren."

"No freight now. Just the one thing." And Wren told it to him with the last of the installments, unhurried, a man spending exactly what he had: "You'll be old when I find you. That's the arithmetic and it can't be helped; growing takes what it takes. So: an old man, and one day at your door there'll be," the breath went and came back, "someone. And you'll know him, and you'll tell yourself you can't possibly, and I want you to remember tonight, this room, me telling you, so that when your whole life argues no, you have one Tuesday that argues," and the sentence went where the sentences of the dying go, and the hand on Dara's wrist stayed where it was, weightless, and some time later, in his own good time, on his own schedule, punctual at last after four hundred years, Wren stopped.

Dara did the death the way the trade does it. Times, forms, the landlady's tea, her tears, her he was no trouble, he was the least trouble of any of them, the room already tidy for exactly this morning. The bag was labeled, in the careful old hand, on a luggage tag gone soft: FOR THE PARAMEDIC.

Inside: the spoil deck, worn round at the corners. The envelopes, all of them, two years of Thursdays, minuted, sealed, banded, in order. The photograph of case eleven. And one envelope he had never seen, sealed, undated, marked in pencil: For later. You'll know when.

He sat in the rig outside the lodging house for eleven minutes with the bag on his knees, and did not open anything, and then he drove back and finished the shift, because the shift was what there was.

The deep years came after that, and they do not take long to tell, which was the horror of them from inside: they did not take long to live, either. They went by like the mainland from the train.

He kept the candle lit in the empty house. He kept the bag on the chair under the window, handles up. He kept the title, and gained another, and gave the retirement speeches of other men and drank the toasts, and stood at the harbor rail some nights with the black water doing its slow work below, and stood there, some nights, longer than a man stands who is only looking at water, and never once put it to himself in the trade's plain terms, though he had taught the terms to nineteen-year-olds: that the checklist had a name for men who stood like that, and that if he had come upon himself on that rail, on any shift of his forty years, he would not have left himself alone with himself.

He was sixty-one, on the harbor road, in the blue hour of a September evening, when he heard it.

Six notes, going up.

A child on a bicycle, no lights, a boy of seven or eight sailing home late along the seawall with his jacket open, humming the way children hum, tunelessly and with total commitment, except that it was not tuneless, it was the air, the exact air, six rising, and Dara stood nailed to the pavement with his heart going like a man post-lift, waiting on the world's whole behalf for the four notes down, and the boy swept past him and around the corner by the chandlery, and the four notes came, faint and failing with distance, four down, and then, at the very edge of hearing, where nobody could swear to anything, going out the floor: the tail.

By the time Dara reached the corner the lane was empty. The streetlight stood in its circle. The strait lay black and patient, and across it, lit for the evening, the thumbnail of the dome kept its watch, and he stood at the corner a long time, an old man at a streetlight, listening to nothing, and the nothing hummed.
