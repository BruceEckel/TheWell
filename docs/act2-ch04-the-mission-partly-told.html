The Well
Act II · Chapter 4

The Mission, Partly Told

The Thursdays changed character after the boat. Wren still brought things, the bag still gave up its plums or its almond biscuits or once, pointedly, a box of good envelopes, but the tea had become a seminar, and Dara took notes, because taking notes was how he kept his footing on any deck, and Wren dictated at the pace of a man who had waited a very long time to be minuted.

"Six came down," Wren said. "Five are still down. You're the sixth."

"Came down when?"

"You, about four hundred years ago, as this side counts it." He said it without ceremony, a date off an inner document, and watched it land. Dara sat with his pen stopped. Four hundred years. He had, until that Thursday, been operating on the private assumption of one previous tenant, the doctor in the newspaper, one life back, an anomaly, a wire in a judgment. The word about arrived like a dispatcher upgrading a call.

"How many," he said. "How many of me."

"More than the doctor," Wren said. "Fewer than you'd fear. I won't give you the number tonight; it isn't information, it's freight, and there's a rule about freight, which is mine, which is the only rule I have: a man gets what he can lift while still walking. You're walking well. Let's keep it so." He turned his mug the quarter turn. "What I'll give you instead is the others, because the others are the job."

And he gave them, one a Thursday sometimes, sometimes two, in the register Dara had come to think of as the fireside gear, low and unhurried, tales with their serial numbers filed off, except that Dara now took the minutes and the tales looked back at him off the page.

There was the one who went down first, Wren said, and went down out of love, which is the worst rope there is because it never frays where you can see it. She went to bring people back, at the start, when they still thought bringing back was a thing the well permitted. And every time she got near the top with someone, there was one more voice below, and she is, to this day, the easiest of them to find and the hardest to move. You could find her yourself by Friday. Go to any town and ask which ward has the night manager who hasn't taken her leave in six years, who knows every patient's grandchildren's names, who the young staff worship and the wise ones worry over. Follow the burnt-out nurses, and at the center of them, burning steadily, her.

There was the one who talks. You've heard of him, probably; he does the halls now, and the radio; I won't say the name tonight because you'll go and listen, and I want you rested. He came down to understand, which was his rope. He understands better than any of us, and he tells it, beautifully, to thousands, and every word is nearly true, and he is the loneliest man alive down here, because he has described the water so well for so long that nobody, least of all him, notices he's still in it.

There was the one who shouts. Her I lose. Whole lifetimes I lose her, and I'm good at finding. She came down angry at the people who wouldn't let us try, and the anger came down with her, and the well does a thing with anger, it gives it a corner and a crowd. What's left of her voice you've heard your whole life, though not from her.

There was the one who made a thing. And the one who refuses, up in the hermit country, whom we will not be troubling; the refusers keep, that's the one mercy of that road; he'll still be refusing when we're ready for him, if we're ever ready for him.

"And him," Dara said, one Thursday, tapping the notebook where the tales had been going. "Me. The sixth. What was his rope?"

Wren held him a long moment in the pale candor, and for the first time in the seminars he looked briefly like a man declining to hand something sharp across a table.

"His rope was that he didn't trust ropes," Wren said. "Which is why he tied his own. You're sitting in it. Next question."

The waking rule came the following week, and Wren delivered it standing at the window, with his back to the room. Dara had learned that the window was where the old man went when the freight was heavy.

"Understand what can't be done, first. Nobody gets carried up the well. It was tried. The ones who tried carrying broke what they carried, and I'll say no more about that tonight or possibly ever, but write the rule and box it: no carrying. A sleeper has to wake down here, all the way down here, inside the life they're standing in, or it isn't waking, it's," and Dara watched the old shoulders search for the word and refuse three candidates, "harm," Wren finished, flatly. "So the job is never to pull. The job is to be there, and to be believed, and to hand a sleeper the one thing that's theirs so unmistakably that the hand remembers what the head can't. Everyone lost down here is one recognition away. The whole trade is in arranging the recognition."

"And between lives," Dara said, keeping his voice level, keeping the pen going, "when it's over, when a life ends. What then. What's there?"

"A landing," Wren said. "A door on it. Open. Lamplight." He said it as simply as a man describing his own hallway. "You've gone past it more times than I'll tell you tonight. Everyone goes past; going past is what the tired do, and everyone arrives there tired, that's the design, if I ever meet the designer we'll discuss it. The whole job, Dara, the whole four hundred years of it, comes down to one thing: walking a man to that landing in such a condition that he notices the door."

The kitchen was quiet. Out the window the strait was doing its evening silver, organized, meant, both.

"What are you," Dara said. "In this. You know the landing like your own hall. You don't shout, you don't preach, you found me twice." He put the pen down, which he had learned was his signal to himself that a question mattered. "Envelope rules. What are you?"

Wren came back to the table and sat down before he answered, and when he answered he looked at his own folded hands.

"A man can't carry a memory down the well," he said. "It washes. You know that better than anyone alive; you've been washed more than anyone alive. But a man, Dara, a whole man, can send a man." He let that stand a moment. "Before he went down, he cut a piece of himself away, a live piece, whole enough to walk and remember and buy boat fares, and he sent it down separately, outside the wash, to keep the count for him. To find him, every go, and stand in front of him, and be so exactly familiar that something below memory says: mine."

The kitchen clock ticked. Dara heard his own voice arrive quietly.

"You're a piece of me."

"I'm a piece of who you are when you're whole," Wren said. "There's a difference, and the difference is most of what I miss." He straightened, and the moment was folded away with the efficiency of four centuries of folding such moments away. "Which piece, you'll want to know. That one's freight. Heaviest I carry, and it goes last, and not because it's cruel. Because it's the one that only helps at the end."

The artifact came on the last Thursday of the month, and it came with a photograph, cheap, glossy, taken decades ago, Wren said, on one of the four days a year the glass came off for the blessing of the holdings: a museum case's innards, tags and velvet, and centered by the photographer's thumb, among censers and cups and a saint's alleged shoe, a coil of grey cord.

Old cord, waxed dark with handling, the length of a man's two spread arms, knotted along its run at even intervals. Nine knots. Plain as rope is plain.

"He made it before he went down," Wren said. "And he made it after a long study of how the well eats things, because it had eaten one already: the one who made a thing, I told you about her, she made hers perfect, and perfect is meat and drink down here, perfect gets a dome built over it. So he sat with her mistake for a year, your man, and then he made the opposite. A thing with no shine on it. No figure, no face, no cup, nothing to kiss, nothing to carry in procession, a thing so plain the church's whole digestion would pass over it. A counting cord, to any eye in any century. Pilgrims used to carry them; there are four hundred in that reliquary; his is the four hundred and first, logged in the seventeen-somethings, provenance unknown, case eleven, third shelf, under a tag that says, and I want you to savor this, because he'd have savored it: tally cord, common, period uncertain."

"And it does what?"

"To me, to you-now, to anyone: nothing. It's cord." Wren leaned forward. "To your hands, the day they hold it: everything. The knots are the count, Dara. The count your fists were keeping the hour you were born, this go and every go. Hands don't wash. That was the whole wager of his life: that the head goes under but the hands come up still gripping, and one day, in some life, on some Keeping Day, the hands and the cord would meet, and the man would remember what he came to do, all of it, at once, the way a house remembers itself when the power comes back on."

Dara sat looking at the photograph. Case eleven. Third shelf. A coil of grey cord under glass in the oldest closed room in the country, an hour across the water, on the far side of a screen, inside a temple, at the top of a stair, in the heart of the church he had spent thirty-four years refusing to enter, guarded by nothing except everything he was.

"The Keeping was open while I was in their library," he said. "I was forty meters from it."

"Yes," Wren said.

"You could have said."

"You couldn't have held it yet," Wren said, without apology. "You'd have been a tourist with a fast heart. There's an order to this, and the order is the only thing I know that the well doesn't: first the believing, then the building, then the cord. We have a year. We'll need it."

Dara looked at the photograph a while longer. Then he turned to a clean page, wrote the header in the block capitals of the trade, and felt the world go simple, or begin to.

JOB: WAKE FIVE. EQUIPMENT: ONE CORD, ONE OLD MAN, ONE TUNE.

"There's a thing you're not telling me," he said, not looking up. "Several, fine, freight, I've boxed the rule. But there's one you nearly told me twice tonight and took back. I watch hands for a living, Wren. Yours went to the bag both times. What's in the bag?"

The silence had a long tail. When Dara looked up, the old man was looking at him with an expression he had no file for: proud, and sorry, and very old.

"The people, Dara," Wren said quietly. "You haven't asked me who the people are. Seven billion of them, down a well. Your mother. Your crews. Everyone wrapped in every blanket you ever tucked. You're a careful man and it's the first question a careful man asks and you haven't asked it, and do you know why you haven't?" He picked up the bag, and stood, and at the door, feet on the sill, he finished it. "Because a piece of you already knows the answer. We'll go and meet them soon, the ones I keep. And after that you can decide what to do about Thursday."

He turned and waved at the corner. Dara stood in the doorway a long time, the notebook hanging from his hand, and across the water, patient under the last of the light, the thumbnail held its place on the horizon, case eleven, third shelf, a year out, keeping.
