# Chapter 11: The Campaign: Havel, and the Cost

The hermit country began two days inland, where the peninsula stood up on end and shed its roads, and the maps called the high part the Harrow, and the Harrow was where refusers had gone since before the Gatherings were gathered: a landscape of wind-bent thorn and standing stone that seemed built to agree with anyone who arrived believing the world should be declined.

The hermit at the spring had been there forty years, this cycle. He took no offerings, which distinguished him from every other holy solitary the Harrow had hosted; pilgrims who left bread found the bread untouched a season later, gone to stone; and the shepherds, who are connoisseurs of refusal, spoke of him with the respect one trade pays another and called him the Dry Man, because he had a spring at his door and was never once seen to be anything but thirsty.

Sefa went up first, by right of ancient quarrel, and was gone three days, and came down the goat path on the evening of the third like weather coming down it, and stood in the shepherd hut's doorway with her fists on her hips.

"Four hundred years," she said, to the room, to the world, to the institution of mountains. "I gave him the whole of it. The waking, the Keeping, the Voice, you, her, the board, the child. I stood on his mountain and I did not shout, Kit, I was reasonable, I built the case like a wall, four hundred years of material and not one stone out of place." She sat down like a placard being laid flat. "And he heard me out. All of it. Never moved. And at the end he opened his eyes and said, *have you finished?*" Her jaw worked. "Four hundred years, and *have you finished*. The man was insufferable in the original body and altitude has not improved him."

"Refusal eats argument," Wren said, mildly, from the fire, not looking up. "I did tell you. It's been eating yours since before the flood; yours is its favorite." He was wrapped in two blankets that he had accepted without comment, which the whole room had noticed and no one had remarked, the balance being what it was. "Go up, Kit. Take nothing he can refuse."

*

So Kit went up the goat path in the first week of winter with a bedroll, a bag of his mother's bread, and no argument at all, and made his camp across the spring from the Dry Man's shelter, at a courteous distance, downwind, the way you'd camp near a wary animal, and set about the only strategy left to the campaign, which was to be there, refusing nothing and asking nothing, one threshold-stander in plain sight of another.

The Dry Man ignored him for eleven days.

It was a masterwork of ignoring, forty years deep in its craft, and Kit, who had grown up being politely not-seen by his own hardened self, recognized every stroke of it and took no offense and hauled his water and said good morning to the weather. He mended, because his hands were his hands: the sprung slats of the spring's old cattle trough, a fallen course of the sheepfold wall. He worked slowly, leaving each thing better and claiming none of it, and on the twelfth day he looked up from the wall to find the hermit standing over him, gaunt as a thorn, with forty years of the Harrow in his face and, far back under it, in the place Kit had learned to look, the smallest handwriting in the world.

"You build like a man who was taught by a mender," the Dry Man said. His voice was dry as the rest, unused, precise. "And you camp like a man who was taught to wait. I know the school. Tell the old contraption I said his apprentice wastes good bread." He looked at the wall, and something crossed the gaunt face, expert and involuntary, at the sight of a course laid true. "The stones are adequate. The visit is refused."

"Noted," Kit said, and kept building.

The first hard storm came in the fifth week, down off the top of the Harrow like something with intent, and it found the weak seam of Kit's camp inside an hour and unmade it: the fly torn, the bedroll soaked, the fire drowned, the temperature going down through the evening like a stone through water. There is a discipline for such nights, and Kit knew it from two trades, and he did the discipline, and the discipline was losing, and somewhere past midnight, with the cold arriving at the place where it stops being weather and becomes arithmetic, Kit Aldan stood up in the flying dark, and walked around the spring, and knocked at the refuser's door.

Not the wind. His knuckles. A knock, at a door the Harrow had known for forty years to be a wall.

It opened. The Dry Man stood in it with a stub of tallow light behind him, and the two refusers of the long story looked at each other through the storm, and Kit said the three words of the lineage, the ones a dying man had needed seventy-eight years and a deathbed to say, the ones that had cost more, in their time, than any confession in the Gatherings, and said them standing, of his own will, at the door of the hardest man alive:

"I am cold."

Something went through the gaunt face like frost cracking. "There's no comfort here," the Dry Man said, the creed, the sign over the door of his whole existence, but his arm had already moved, the door already stood wider, and Kit stepped in past him into the one dry room in the Harrow, and was given, with a grudging ferocity that was its own dialect of tenderness, the fire, the second blanket, the soup the hermit would not keep for himself. And Kit took it. That was the whole of the strategy, and it was not a strategy (the storm had seen to that): he sat in the refuser's one chair in the refuser's blanket and ate the refuser's soup, shaking, undestroyed, and said thank you, once, plainly, and did not perform it, and did not apologize for it, and the Dry Man stood against the far wall with his arms wrapped in his own arms and watched a fellow refuser lay it down, and live, watched it the way men watch a bridge hold under a load the engineering said it could not hold.

"It doesn't cost what the ledger says," Kit said, into the fire, when the shaking had gone. "That's the finding. Forty years up here, four hundred down there, all of us keeping the same book, and the number in it is wrong, Havel. I've tested it. Being helped doesn't spend you. I sat in a ward once, being nursed like a child, and I came out with more of myself, not less. The book is wrong, and you wrote our copy, and you read it aloud twice, and I've come up this mountain in winter to file the correction."

The tallow burned. The storm worked the roof.

"You know the readings," the man said. Not a question.

"Item eleven," Kit said, from a room four hundred years up the well, with the racks humming. "*That the self, under repeated immersion, will not retain integrity of purpose; that dissolution of the operator must be assumed total; that no protocol herein provides for recovery.* You read it twice. You made us hear it twice. Smallest handwriting on the page, last name in the column, and then, Havel, then you signed." Kit leaned forward, into the light, and asked it, the question the mountain had been built to prevent: "You were right about all of it. We drowned, every one, exactly as read. So why did you sign?"

The refuser stood against his wall in the guttering light, and the mask, worn so long it had done what masks do and become a face, worked once, twice, and failed, and underneath it was the smallest handwriting in the world, and it said what it had always said, in the ink the whole rigor had existed to hide.

"Because you were all going," Havel Iyer said, "and I could not warn you from the shore." And then, in the voice of a man reading his own marginalia after four centuries: "There's your item twelve. The one I never wrote down. That the operator's attachment to the other operators is not dissoluble. That it survives immersion. That it is, of everything tested, the one thing that always comes up still gripping." He looked at his hands, wrapped in his own arms, holding on to the only person they had permitted themselves in forty years. "I didn't stay up here to refuse the world, boy. Any shepherd can refuse the world. I stayed up here because I was right, and being right had a body count, and I could not sit in a town full of drowning pieces of the five people I," the dry voice stopped, and made itself finish, "of the five people. And feed on comfort. While you drowned."

"We're not drowning anymore," Kit said. "Come down the mountain, item twelve. The correction's filed. The five people want their sixth."

*

The counting was done in the fabric workshop, in the spring, with all of them.

HAVEL, crossed through, in his own hand, still the smallest on any page: *descended. resuming. the readings were sound; the conclusion was not.* He had come down in March, gaunt and blinking, and been fed at the north door among the needy, as one of them, which he had insisted on, reading the Rule's intake paragraph with a scholar's grim approval; and the order, nine centuries practiced at absorbing the Harrow's returners, had taken him into the library, where the collation project acquired a reviewer of terrifying rigor and the librarian aged a decade in a season.

THE MAKER they did not cross through, because there was nothing to cross. Sefa had taken the question up the whole coast that winter, and Havel had taken it through the records, and the line stood at the end of it as it had stood for four centuries: *sought.* "Makers are the worst finding there is," Wren said, when the pencil hovered and moved on. "The rest of us leak. We cry at gates, we teach in halls, we answer bells; a searcher has something to hear. A maker pours the whole of herself into the made thing and closes the lid, and the thing keeps her, and things don't weep. I have stood in front of work in nine cities that stopped my breath, Kit, joins no eye could find, and never once known, not once in four hundred years, whether I was looking at masonry or at Noor."

And then the last line. THE CHILD BY THE WINDOW.

Ansa had found him; her network was wards, and every town had a ward, and word had come back in the winter: a boy in Aldermoor, nine years old now, a fisher family's fostered nephew, healthy, ordinary, well loved. Who would not sleep unless the window stood open, in any weather, in that climate; who hummed no tune anyone had taught him; and who had asked his schoolteacher, that autumn, a question she had written to her sister about because it would not leave her: *whether a person could be homesick for a house they had never been in.*

The workshop was quiet. Aldermoor: Corin's city, the doctor's streets, the drawer that had held two letters arguing in the dark. The well reused everything; nothing was wasted; nobody was finished.

"Nine," Wren said, from the bench, wrapped and small. "The window's still open, then. In every sense. But the arithmetic is the arithmetic: it can't be told to a nine-year-old by strangers, it can't be hurried, and it can't be me. My freight goes to one address." He looked around the room, at the crew of them, and said the plain thing. "Someone has to take the long watch. Not this life only. His next, and the one after, however many it wants. The boy will grow, and forget, and go down his own wells, and someone with the whole count in her head has to be there, at the gates of him, life after life, the way I was at yours. It's the worst posting the enterprise has. It's decades of standing in the rain being taken for mad. I held it four hundred years and I'll tell you what it costs and what it pays, and the costs are itemizable and the payment is one sentence long: one morning, at some gate, in some body, the face comes out of the light, and it opens."

"It's mine," Ansa said.

No one had doubted it; the room turned to her anyway; and she took off the reading glasses, and folded them, and gave the campaign her terms with the unhurried care of a woman who had spent a year learning the difference between a summons and a choice.

"Four hundred years I went down because I couldn't not," she said. "The bell rang and I was in the chair; you've heard the story; I've been serving the sentence for it since. Well. I've sat a year with my own difficult patient, and here's what we found at the bottom of the ward: I can not go. That's new, all of you. That's the whole of what waking bought me: I could stay up top when my body's done, walk through my own door on my own landing, and rest, and it would be no betrayal, and nobody would drown for it, and knowing that," she looked at each of them, one round of the room, bed by bed, "knowing I could stay is the first thing in four centuries that makes the going free. So I'll go. For the talker. For the boy by the window. Not because a child cried in a machine, not because I rang anyone down, not because I can't hear a bell without running. Because I've weighed my rest against his gates, awake, on a fair scale, and I choose the gates." She put the glasses in her pocket, and picked up the pencil, and wrote it on the board herself, beside the child's line, in the chart-hand that had steadied nine centuries of dying strangers: *taken. A.S. Freely.*

It was the first entry of its kind in the history of the world below: a descent with a clean signature.

The campaign stood at that, and they knew it: two woken, one descended and resumed, one growing by a window in Aldermoor, expected, not to be hurried. Incomplete, and true, the way progress was, a knot's worth in a life; and that evening they cleared the bench and played spoil, five-handed, the mender and the baker's son and the infirmarian and the shouter and the scholar down off his mountain, and the game went long, and the light went, and Wren, dealt a hand anyone could have won with, played it with four centuries of craft and lost, roundly, to Sefa, to the table's disbelieving roar, and sat back in his blankets amid the noise of them, the crew of them, the found and woken family of the whole long enterprise, and laughed the way the very old laugh, silently, entirely, with everything, a man at the end of a shift watching the relief walk in with the lamps already lit.
