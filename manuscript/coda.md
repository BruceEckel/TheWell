# Coda

Far up the well of the years there is a sealed building, and in the sealed building there is a room where machines hum in racks, and the machines notice first.

They have kept this room a long time, by their own patient measures: kept the air at the right softness, kept the six cradles and what the cradles hold, kept the lamp over the inner door burning, because the last hand out of the world set it so and no instruction has ever countermanded it. Now the readouts move. The machines do what they were built to do, which is everything, gently; and in the small hours of an ordinary morning, in a facility that does not officially exist, three of the six cradles open.

The bodies are not old. That is the arithmetic of the well, and even the ones rising from it lie still a while, doing the sums: four hundred years of lifetimes below, and above, in this room, among these racks, the tea in the abandoned mug on the real-wood table has not yet fully soured. Weeks, the readouts say. It has been weeks.

Sefa is up first, of course, swearing softly at her own knees in a language her mouth remembers better than she does. Havel rises the way he signed, last, carefully, checking each reading twice. And the third body sits up slowly in its cradle, and flexes its hands, and looks at them for a long time, palms and backs, the hands of a man of the future world, unweathered, uncalloused, hands that have never in this body laid a course of stone or wrapped a roadside blanket or tied off a dressing in a moving rig, and that know, all the way down, below memory, in the stuff that does not wash, how to do every one of those things.

Ren Ashwin gets up and makes the tea.

He makes it without thinking, which is the point, though nobody in the room knows to see it: fills the machine, finds the mugs, and sets one down at Sefa's elbow and one by Havel's checking hands, unasked, exactly, without ceremony, the way things were set down beside four of his lifetimes by everyone who ever loved him; and the piece of him that learned it first, the piece that carried it down and up again through eleven bodies and four centuries, is not standing across the room in a green cardigan, and is not gone. He is in the hands. He is in the pouring and the placing and the not-asking. He is home, the way a rescued thing is home, indistinguishable at last from the man who sent him, and when Sefa takes the mug and looks up, something she sees in Ren's face makes her go still a moment, and then nod, once, the crew's nod, and say nothing, there being nothing that needs saying to a man so plainly in one piece.

Three cradles stay closed.

The machines keep them the way the order keeps the Keeping: without hurry, without doubt, on standing instruction. In one, the body of a man who is at this moment a boy in Aldermoor, sleeping under an open window. In one, the body of a woman whose whereabouts below are marked on a certain board with one old word, kept alive as a promise. And in one, the body of the team's heart, tended, warm, its breathing slow as tides; its occupant elsewhere, far down, on the longest watch there is, by her own hand, freely. Ren stands before her cradle a while with his tea, and touches the glass, once, the way you check bread, and leaves her to her round.

Then the three of them go to the outer door, and open it, and step out into the future.

It is exactly where they left it, and they stand on the threshold taking it in: the padded world, six weeks older, soft as a ward at midnight, the world that would not buy sky with pain, going on about its comfortable business under a sky it has forgotten to look at. Somewhere in its records there is a tribunal's finding, weeks old, ink barely settled: *the meeting is closed*. Somewhere in a perfect chair, in perfect light, a woman is sitting who has not stood up for eleven years, who wants to want, and weeps without salt, and is waiting, though she does not know it, for the longest-delayed appointment in the history of medicine.

They have been gone six weeks. They are four hundred years old. They have brought back the one thing the future lacks, and it fits in no container and shows on no readout: weather, carried in three bodies, the whole harvest of the well, and the knowledge, tested to the last knot, that the fire does not hate the ore.

There is a hearing to request. There is a woman in a chair. Sefa is already composing her opening sentence; Havel is already correcting it. Ren Ashwin closes the facility door behind them, gently, and does not lock it, and they go down the soft corridor of the future three abreast, unhurried, awake, and the lamp over the inner door burns on behind them, in policy, in certainty, for whichever of them ever comes up.

And below, meanwhile, always, now:

A city. Any city; this one has a harbor and a night bus. The woman on the bus is fifty-one and has been cleaning offices since six in the evening, and her feet have stopped reporting to her, and the bag on her knees holds a uniform for tomorrow and a form from the school she has read twice without absorbing and an apple she was too tired to eat. The flat will be dark when she gets in. Her son sleeps with his lamp on; the bill for that is in the bag too; everything is in the bag. It has been a long year, of a long stretch of them, and nothing about tomorrow says otherwise, and she rides with her head against the glass, watching the shut shops go by, the way the tired watch, which is barely watching.

The driver is humming. Some old air, six notes going up and four coming down, with a little fall at the end of it that seems to go down through the floor of the bus; he probably doesn't know he's doing it; drivers hum. She doesn't know the tune. She doesn't notice she's listening.

But somewhere between one stop and the next, for no reason she could name, the night eases.

Not the feet; the feet are honest; not the bag, which weighs what it weighs. Something else, underneath, the thing that has been clenched so long she stopped counting it as clenched, lets go by some small degree, the way a held breath ends, and the shut shops going by look, briefly, not like the end of anything but like the middle of something: a long street with lit windows further on. It lasts a minute, maybe two. It is not hope exactly, and it is not rest exactly, and if anyone had asked she would have said she was only tired, and she would have been right; she is only tired; it has cost her everything to be this tired; and for a minute, riding through the dark of the world with her head on the glass, the cost feels, without a single reason on earth, like it is being counted somewhere. Like it appears in some column, after all. Like nothing is wasted.

Her stop. She gathers the bag. She thanks the driver, which she does not always have the wherewithal to do, and he nods, mid-hum, and the doors sigh shut behind her and the bus carries its small lit room away up the street.

The flat is dark, and warm, and her son has fallen asleep with the lamp on again, one arm flung out over a book about engines. She stands in his doorway a moment, the way mothers stand in doorways, and does not turn off the lamp. Let it burn tonight. It is a small cost. She washes, and sets out the uniform, and signs the school form at the kitchen table without reading it a third time, trusting the world an inch further than the evidence, because you have to, because everyone does, because that is the whole trade.

And before she sleeps, last thing, no reason, the way her grandmother used to and her grandmother never said why, she opens the bedroom window one clean centimeter, for the air.

Then she sleeps. Toward morning the rain arrives, soft, unhurried, asking nothing, and the open window lets it be heard.
