# Chapter 1: The Recurrence

Dara Marek was born in Lorne, where the ferries left from, which the town said about itself so often that it had stopped being geography and become apology. Across the water, an hour on the boat when the strait behaved, was Sareth: the dome visible on clear days from the harbor road, a coin-colored thumbnail on the horizon that every kitchen window in town kept an accidental eye on, the way kitchens elsewhere keep an eye on a mountain.

His mother cleaned rooms at the Strand Hotel and believed. His father worked the vehicle deck of the Sareth ferry twenty-six years and believed nothing except weather, which on that crossing was belief enough. His grandmother lived up the hill with a wood stove and the quiet hour on Thursdays and a card game called spoil that she claimed to have learned on a boat, and the boy went up to her after school, and she beat him at spoil for six years straight without once appearing to hurry, and he loved her the way you love the one person who never asks how you are.

He was, everyone agreed, a good boy with habits.

He stood in doorways. His mother had a photograph of him at four, asleep upright, wedged into the frame of the kitchen door like a dropped broom, and the story that went with it got told at every family thing until he learned to leave the room, which he did by way of the doorway, where he would, if not watched, stop. The boy stands in thresholds like he's paid to, his father said, not unkindly. In any room he could tell you the doors, the number of them, which ones stuck. He hummed, from before he could have learned it anywhere, one particular air, six notes up and four down, over homework, in the bath, under the racket of the gulls on the seawall, and when his mother finally asked where he'd got it he said it came with the radio, which became one of the family's phrases for anything of unknown origin: it came with the radio.

And he had no time for the church. That arrived early too, before he could argue it, a weather system of its own. In Lorne this was harder work than it would have been elsewhere. The Ostrine faith in the capital's old country wasn't an opinion, it was plumbing: the year had Keeping Days in it the way it had a solstice, the ferry ran pilgrim fares, half the town wore the cup somewhere about them, and Dara's mother kept a shelf with a candle that was never allowed to go fully out, a discipline she performed with the calm of a woman keeping a small fire for someone expected home. Eight-year-old Dara asked her, in front of guests, who checked the candle when the whole family died in the night and nobody came, which was the kind of question he asked, and she looked at him the long way she had and said, then the neighbors light theirs, love, and he had no answer to that, and minded not having one, and went and stood in the doorway.

The school trip went to Sareth in the spring he was nine.

Every school in the country made the crossing eventually; it was in the curriculum, between the salt marsh and the mint. The Cradle received schoolchildren on Tuesdays, crocodile file, worksheets, a teacher counting heads at the arch. They climbed the Long Stair in the rain, and Dara's feet found the groove worn down its center and stayed in it the whole way up, which the worksheet did not have a box for, and then the blind corner turned and the temple square opened and the building stood up in front of them all at once, the way it had stood up in front of everyone for nine centuries, and the class said the things children say, and they went in through the great doors in their wet coats, and somewhere between the doors and the far screen, without warning, without stages, in the gold-brown dark with the candles standing in it, Dara began to cry.

Not the crying of a lost child. The other kind, the kind with no content, water finding a seam. He stood in the nave of the Cradle of Sareth at nine years old with tears running level and silent down a completely bewildered face, and the teacher crouched to his height and asked what was wrong, and he said, accurately, nothing, and could not stop, and did not know the thing he would have needed to know to stop, which was what had opened, or when it had ever been shut, or by whom. They took him out to the square. In the ordinary light it closed again, whatever it was, closed like water behind a wake, and left him soaked and furious. On the ferry home the class had already made the word for him, cryba, criers' names being the fastest industry in any school, and it lasted a term, and it did what such names do: it went down into the foundations and set.

He never told anyone what he decided on that crossing, being nine, and not knowing he was deciding. But the shape of the rest of him can be read off it the way a wall can be read off its first course: whatever that building had done to him, it had done without permission, from outside, through channels no one had consented to, and a thing that can reach into a boy and turn a tap he cannot find again afterward is not a comfort, it is a power, and powers that will not show their instruments were to be, from that Tuesday forward, his enemy. The church called the feeling grace. He had a different name for anything that got in without knocking.

The vocation arrived the way his vocations of every life arrived, disguised as an accident.

He was sixteen, on the harbor road on a Saturday in October, when a linkspan chain let go on the vehicle deck ramp and a car came down wrong with a man half out of it. Twenty people stood and watched it happen, and afterward, when the town told the story, it told it the same way every time: and the Marek boy walked forward. He did not remember walking forward. He remembered the world going simple. Airway, pressure, don't move the neck, send that one specific gawker to phone and that one to the ferry office for the trauma kit, his voice coming out level and already institutional, a sixteen-year-old distributing the emergency to bystanders like a man dealing cards, and underneath the simplicity, rising, unlooked-at, something that had been waiting his whole short life for the world to finally need what he was: this is the job. Not a job. The job. The one true church of the show-up.

The man lived, minus a foot. The town called Dara a hero for a fortnight, which he treated as a rash that would pass. He kept the other thing: the going simple. He chased it into a technician's course at eighteen, out of the classroom onto the trucks at twenty, and was by twenty-five the paramedic that the dispatchers, who ration their compliments like morphine, called sound. Sound was the word. In seven years on the peninsula's roads he had acquired the reputation that follows certain responders around like a dog: that calls went better when Marek was on, that the panic in a room dropped three degrees when he came through the door, that he could tell you without looking how many exits any building had, that he hummed, six notes up and four down, while he worked the worst of it, and that the humming, colleagues swore, did something, settled something, though nobody could have said in whom.

He gave the church its due precisely once a shift, in the paperwork sense: the forms had a box for it, faith support requested, and he ticked it when the patient asked, and made the radio call, and said nothing. What he thought lived one layer down and everyone worked with him long enough learned its shape. The speakers who came to the bad calls in their good coats, who stood in farm kitchens saying the fire does not hate the ore over women whose sons he had just failed to restart: he watched them the way you watch a rival crew work a wreck with the wrong tools. Elegantly. Confidently. Wrong. Pain was not a craftsman, pain was cargo, and his whole calling was the moving of it, off chests, out of airways, down graded doses, away, and a doctrine that dressed the cargo up as freight-paid was, he said once, drunk, at a station barbecue, the longest con in history because it billed the crash victims for the crash. Somebody said, you sound like you've given this thought, Marek. Somebody else said, leave it, he's got form, his lot are from Lorne. General laughter, his included. His bark of it, one note, that the station did impressions of.

He lived alone, by policy, in a flat above the chandlery with a kitchen window that kept, without his consent, the accidental eye on the horizon's coin-colored thumbnail. Women arrived and left on a schedule they eventually recognized as structural: he was, they reported, with an accuracy that never reached him, magnificent in a crisis and missing in a kitchen; he would come off a nightmare shift and permit no hand on him, would stand instead, they all noticed it, they compared notes at the one wedding two of them attended, in the doorway of whatever room she was in, one shoulder on the frame, close, present, unreachable, a man visiting his own life through a hatch.

And the blanket thing. That got noticed too, by crews, by patients' families, though it was such a small thing: Marek's tuck. Survivors on the roadside, shocky, teeth going; he'd come out of the truck with the blanket already unfolded and put it around them in exactly three movements, two at the shoulders and a third at the feet if they were sitting, fast, unceremonious, complete, a competence of the hands so total it required nothing of the person inside it, no consent, no thanks, no transaction at all, and more than one stranger wrapped that way on the worst night of their life was surprised by sudden tears and put it down to the crash. He did it, colleagues estimated, a thousand times in a career. Where he'd got it nobody asked. It came with the radio.

He was thirty-four, sound, humming, alone above the chandlery, when the stranger found him.
