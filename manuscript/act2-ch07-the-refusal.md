# Chapter 7: The Refusal

The winter took Wren down a flight at a time.

By December the stick was load-bearing and the pause at the half landing had become two pauses, renamed as interest in the harbor and then not renamed at all. The cough had moved in below the fogs that had brought it. Dara ran the assessment now openly, professionally, wrist and nail beds and the count of the breathing, and Wren submitted to it with the amusement of a man letting a boy check a boat he himself had built, and when Dara said the word *ward*, said it the way the trade says it, flat and load-rated, Wren said, "I know exactly what this is, and roughly what it gives me, and the answer is spring, and we will not be spending the spring in a corridor with a curtain." He patted Dara's hand where it held his wrist, twice, an old man's tap. "I've died more times than you've restocked that rig, Dara. It's the commute. But this stretch of road matters, so." He settled back. "We're going to stop being careful, you and I. I'm running out of Thursdays, and the bag has one thing left in it, and it's the heavy one, and tonight you're going to help me lift it."

Outside, the strait ran black under the early dark. Dara made the tea and did not sit.

"You asked me once who the people are," Wren said. "And then you were wise, and never asked again. Ask now."

"Who are the people, Wren."

And Wren told him.

"Six came down," he said. "You have that. What you don't have is what we came down into. There was nobody here, Dara. Not at the start. The well was a built thing full of built people, and the built people didn't work, they were furniture with faces, the way a drawn fire doesn't warm a drawn room. It was the first thing everyone learned and the last thing anyone accepted: you cannot study a person by building a doll of one. So they, we, did the other thing." The old hands folded. "A person is not one thing. You know this; it's your own trade's open secret; you've heard a mother be four people in one bad hour on a roadside. A person is a parliament. Pieces. Parts. The grief that never got to speak, the child that keeps the accounts, the sentry that will not come off the wall. And a part, Dara, a part is not a doll. A part is alive. It wants, it fears, it holds one wound and holds it perfectly, forever, waiting for its turn to be heard. So when the built people failed, the six of us did the thing that worked, and God and the record forgive us, it worked beyond all measure." He looked up. "We peopled the well out of ourselves. We let our parts out of us and gave them bodies. Every one of them carrying its one wound, which is to say its one question, and each of them drawn to the others' wounds like water to low ground, and they married, Dara, they quarreled and built towns and had children, and the children, the children of two embodied wounds are wounds themselves, new ones, compounded, and those children had children. Four hundred years at the interest that suffering pays. Do the arithmetic you're already doing and stop halfway; it doesn't help.

"Your mother. Your crews. The canon. The woman on the salt road's forty residents and the woman on the salt road, all of it, all of them, everyone you have ever wrapped in a blanket or failed to restart, every face at every window of every town you've driven through at four in the morning: pieces of six people, ground fine, and every piece alive, and every piece hurting, because hurting is what a part is, a part is a hurt that got a body, and there is no one else down here. That's who the people are. There was never anyone else down here."

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked. Dara stood with his back against the counter and his arms folded, and he had gone, Wren observed, exactly as still as his own file said he went, the stillness before the world goes simple, except that nothing was going simple.

"Why," Dara said, at last, and his voice was level, and the level was the warning. "Why would anyone build that. Say the why."

"Because it heals," Wren said, not looking away. "Because it was the only thing that ever healed. That's the why, Dara, that was always the why, there is no other why underneath it, I've looked. A wound talked about stays a wound; your own dead doctor knew it and was struck off for it. But a wound lived, all the way through, in a body, in a life, with weather and children and rent, a wound that gets its turn to be a whole person and make its mistake and see, at last, from inside, what its mistake costs, that wound closes. Slowly. A little in a lifetime, a knot's worth in a life. It is the slowest medicine ever compounded, and it is the only one that has ever worked on what we are, and the six of us knew our world was dying of the painlessness, of the sedation, of every wound wrapped and none ever opened. And so it was built, and we came down into it, and we brought our wounds with us because there was no leaving them at the top, we ARE them, and every life down here, Dara, every single one, your mother's, the canon's, that man dying at Saltmead with his tide, every life is a wound getting its turn. Getting its turn. That's what suffering is, down here. It isn't punishment and it isn't malfunction and it isn't the fire being fire. It's a turn being taken." The old voice did not rise; it did the other thing, it came down to the register of the very end of freight. "It is mercy, Dara. Slow, and blind, and priced in a coin nobody consented to be paid in, and I have watched it work for four hundred years, and it is mercy."

Dara set his mug down. He did it carefully, which was the second warning, the care of a man handling something at a scene.

"Say the last part again."

"Which part."

"Priced in a coin nobody consented to be paid in." His voice stayed level one more sentence's worth. "You've just told me every person on this earth is in the fire, and the fire is on purpose, and the purpose is their own good, and they never signed for it. That's not freight, Wren. I know that one. I've known that one since I was eight years old in a chapel with the wrong number of windows. That is the sermon. That is the oldest, best-dressed, most-translated cruelty in the history of this world or apparently any other: it hurts because it's healing you, hold still in the burning. My whole life," and the level broke, not upward into shouting, downward, into something worse, the flat fast voice of the parapet, "my whole life I have held one line. One. The pain is not for anything. That's the line. That's the whole of what I have instead of your church: when I kneel on a road in the rain holding some shattered kid, I do not owe him a meaning, I owe him morphine. The one thing I have never done, the one thing, is lean down into his face and tell him this is good for you. And now you sit in my kitchen, in her chair," where that came from he didn't know, there was no *her chair*, there had never been a *her chair*, "and you tell me the kid is a piece of me, and the road is a machine I helped build, and the crash is therapy. You're not bringing me out of the sermon, Wren. You're bringing me the sermon with schematics."

"Dara."

"I'm not the fireman." He heard his own voice arrive at it, the place it had been going all his life, the address on the letter he had carried unopened for thirty-six years. "That's what you came to tell me. Thursday after Thursday, plums, envelopes, four hundred years of patience, all of it to walk me gently to this: I'm not the fireman, I'm the arsonist. Six of us lit it. Seven billion people are burning in a fire I lit, my mother is burning in it, and she lights a candle every night to the mercy of the thing that's burning her, and the candle, the shelf, the cup, the whole apparatus of her comfort was grown by the fire to keep the burning bearable, and you want me to finish the job the arsonists started because it would be," he could not say the word and then said it, "merciful."

"That is almost exactly right," Wren said, steadily, "and entirely wrong, and the difference between those,"

"Did she consent?" Dara said.

The kitchen went silent.

"The parts, Wren. The pieces. You said it yourself: nobody asked them. The six volunteered; fine; six adults signed for six adults, and then they signed for everyone. Every part let out of us was let out, not asked out. My mother is a wound with a body, you tell me, some splinter of one of six strangers, taking her turn in a machine she never saw the outside of, and when she was frightened at night as a girl, which she was, she told me, she prayed, and the praying was the machine too. Where in your four hundred years of freight, in your whole bag, old man, is her signature?"

And Wren, for the first time in two years of Thursdays, had nothing in the bag.

He sat with his hands folded and took it, all of it, with his eyes open, and what was in his face was not defeat and was not patience, it was recognition, the specific grief of a man hearing a voice he knows come out of a door he has stood in front of many, many times, and when he finally spoke he did not answer the question, because there are questions that are not answered but outlived, and he was out of lifetime to outlive this one in.

"You're going to ask me to leave now," he said. "I'd like to pre-empt one thing before you do, because you'll spend years on it otherwise: nothing you have said tonight is stupid, or small, or wrong in its bones. It is the best of you talking. It's the sentry that will not come off the wall, and I have loved that sentry since before you were born, any of the times you were born. But hear one sentence as I go, and you don't have to keep it, just let it into the hall: the fact that a thing has always been said by the cruel does not prove it can never be said by the kind."

"Get out of my kitchen," Dara said.

Wren stood, slowly, joints filing their paperwork, and took up the canvas bag, and at the door he paused, feet on the sill, on the join in the boards, exactly where he had stood at the end of every Thursday for two years, and he did not say the thing about the corner, and he did not say Thursday, and he went down the stairs one flight at a time into the dark and the rain.

Dara stood at the window over the sink, where the strait ran black and the far shore showed nothing, the thumbnail swallowed hours ago by the weather, and he watched the small stooped shape go along the lane under the one streetlight, stick first, and stop at the corner.

And turn. And raise one hand in the rain, the same as every Thursday, holding it up a long moment, a pale mark against the dark, patient, faithful, uninvoiced.

Dara shut the door of the room before the hand came down. It was the first time in two years he had not watched the corner until it was empty, and the click of the latch was very loud, and the flat was very quiet, and he stood in the middle of it, in his station of a life, boots by the door, kettle fast, one window that mattered and nothing at all in it now but weather, and he did not hum, that night, or for a long time after.
