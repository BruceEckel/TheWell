# Chapter 6: The Novice

The Keepers received seekers on the first Monday of every month, at the north door, in the queue.

That was the first cost, and Wren had not softened it: there was no side entrance for the proud. Aspirants stood in the same line as the bread line, the destitute and the called shuffling forward together in the cold under the same lintel, because the Rule, nine centuries old, said that no one entered the Cradle's service except among the needy, as one of them, and the lane watched, because the lane always watched. Kit stood in that queue on a Monday in October with his breath smoking, between an old man with his belongings in two carrier bags and a girl who wept steadily and ate a roll at the same time, and halfway up the line he saw, at the arch where the onlookers stood, his mother and Lena, come to see it, as families came, and his mother's face was doing something complicated and blazing that he had no name for, and Lena was crying with her chin up, furious about it, and Kit, twenty-seven years old, publicly, in the sight of the whole Stair that had known him as the boy who saw through everything, took one step at a time toward the door he had spent a life refusing, in the queue, seen, one of them.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done. It took eleven minutes. Afterward he could never explain, even to Wren, which of those two facts was the stranger.

The master of novices, Keeper Brant, conducted the interview in a room with two chairs of equal height. He was seventy and dry as a bell rope, and read Kit's papers, the mainland degree, the six print-works years, with the expression of a man pricing a horse.

"And what do you believe?" Brant said, and then, before Kit's mouth had finished opening, "That was a joke. We don't ask. Belief is the one thing this building never asks anyone to prove; it can't be proved; the Rule calls demanding it usury." He set the papers down. "What I'm required to establish is simpler. Most men arrive here carrying something. Grief, usually. Sometimes worse. Once in a while a man arrives carrying anger so old it has become his skeleton, and he thinks the robe will hide it, and he thinks, forgive me, that we have not seen him coming for nine hundred years." The old eyes came up, mild and entirely awake. "Anger is a door. It's listed in the Rule as one; I can show you the paragraph. But a door is for going through, novice, not for living in. You'll be asked to work, to keep the hours, to kneel with the rest at the offices, and to be, in this house, exactly what you are, which appears to be," he glanced at the papers again, "an engineer who despises us and has run out of everywhere else. Well. We've built with worse. The stones of this place were every one of them laid by somebody's grief; despair is load-bearing here; you'll fit right in." And he stamped the form with the little cup-shaped seal, and said the words that admitted seekers, which were not a blessing and were nine centuries old: "Come in, then. Mind the step."

*

The life was the life. Rise in the dark to the bells, which he learned were rung, by hand and by rote, by a keeper called Tomm who was simple and serene and had rung them for forty years; offices, work, offices, work, the day scaffolded by observance the way his mainland days had been scaffolded by sessions, which he noted, in the ledger he still kept behind his eyes, in the column reserved for resemblances nobody wanted pointed out.

They gave him to the fabric, because his papers said engineer and the Cradle was nine centuries of deferred maintenance wearing a dome. The fabric workshop sat behind the east range, stone-floored, tool-hung, smelling of oil and beeswax and cold iron, and the first morning he stood in it among the benches and the mended things and had to put a hand on the doorframe, because it was, to the smell and the light and the arrangement of its patience, the workshop at the top of the lane, scaled up by a power of ten and staffed by robes: the same trade, the same grammar, mending what came. The master of fabric looked at Kit's hands, gave him a corroded window-winder from the clerestory, and said, *see what you make of that*, and by evening Kit had it moving like a young thing, and the master grunted, and that was the interview that mattered.

Within a season he held keys. Fabric held keys to everywhere (that was fabric's dark joke: the mice and the mechanics go where they please); he wound the tower clock; he greased the funicular's brake governor, his own machined part, fitted at sixteen in another life of his life; and on the middle mornings of the great processions it was the fabric novice, by rota, who climbed to the loft above the great doors and, at eleven minutes before the hour, by a brass timer worn smooth by two centuries of thumbs, released the incense into the channels that fed the nave.

He had clocked the trick at sixteen from the square with a stopwatch. At twenty-seven he stood in the loft with his thumb on the lever and was the trick, and the first time he did it he stood afterward in the dark among the ducts, listening to the crowd-sound change below as the scent hit them, that soft collective loosening, thousands of shoulders coming down at once, and he catalogued it with the old contempt, conditioning, a bell rung for dogs, and the contempt performed its function flawlessly, and something underneath the contempt, in the new column, wrote quietly: *they climbed a hill for this. Somebody meets them at the top. You are the somebody now.* And he could not make the entry balance, and filed it open, which he was learning, in that house, to do.

Because that was the thing the mainland doctrine had not prepared him for and the lane contempt had not prepared him for: the order knew. That was the scandal of the inside. He had expected either fools who believed the machinery holy or cynics who ran it cold, and found instead, at the weekly chapter where the processions were planned like harbor traffic, candle margins and crowd flow and the hostel's linen budget discussed in the same dry breath as the care of the dying, men and women who knew exactly what the incense timer was and ran it anyway, on purpose, the way his mother ran the ovens: because the people were coming, and the people were tired, and the machinery of comfort, run honestly, was called hospitality, and, run dishonestly, was called what he had always called it, and the difference, said Keeper Brant once, in chapter, without looking at anyone, was not in the machinery. It was in what you charged.

*

The other column filled at the north door and the infirmary.

The bread line went out at dawn, every dawn, nine centuries deep, his mother's rule wearing stone: no one turned away, no questions asked at the door, and fabric novices took their rota there too, ladling, carrying, fetching. He had grown up feeding pilgrims across a counter for coin; now he set soup down in front of men who could pay nothing, and learned the door's craft from a fat old keeper named Salt who had done it thirty years: *you don't ask, you don't hover, you don't bless them unless they ask you first. Set it down and move along. The gift with the invoice still attached is a bill, son.* And Kit, hearing the whole grammar of his life pronounced as a soup-line instruction, stood with his ladle in the steam and had to look very hard at the pot.

It worked. That was the fact of the second column, and he made himself record it with the rigor he had once spent on the first: it worked. Not on everyone, not always, nothing worked on everyone always. But he watched a man his own age, grey with the specific exhaustion Kit knew now to look for, sit motionless over a bowl set down without ceremony, and begin, at the third spoonful, to cry into it, exactly the way the wrapped survivors on the peninsula's roadsides had cried, and he understood that the crying was not about the soup and had never been about the soup, and that whatever the church upstairs said about doors and lamps, down here at the north door it practiced a wordless craft he had seen before, in a bakery, in a workshop, in the cab of a rig he had never driven, in every hand that had ever set a thing down beside him without asking: the uninvoiced gift, performed at scale, under a dome, for nine hundred years, by an institution that also ran an incense timer, and both were true, and both were it, and the ledger would not close.

The infirmary he came to by way of a broken autoclave, and stayed for by way of rota, and it was there he first took proper notice of Keeper Ansa.

She ran the pilgrim infirmary the way, he thought, harbors are run in countries with good coastguards. Fiftyish, cardiganed under her robe against the stone cold, reading glasses up in her grey hair; she knew every bed's name and every name's story, the dying pilgrim's granddaughter's exam results, the old sailor's horror of the dark, and she moved down the ward at a pace that had no hurry and lost no time, and the young novices worshipped her, and the older keepers, he noticed, watched her with a particular quality of concern, the way you watch a lamp burn that you cannot refill. She had not taken her rest-year, Salt told him, in eleven years; the order's joke, worn smooth, was that Ansa would take her rest when the sea did. Kit mended her autoclave, and her ward-bell circuit, and the wheel of a drug trolley that had squeaked, she said, since the old Keeper's time, and she thanked him each time with a warmth that was entirely real and entirely impersonal, the warmth of a fire that warms whoever stands there, and some old instrument in him, some inheritance he had stopped arguing with, filed her, unbidden, under a heading he didn't know he had: the ones who are always easy to find, and the hardest to move.

He mentioned her to Wren, in the fabric yard, on a visiting day, among the benches where the mender, old and slow and looked for by everyone, the lane's ambassador, now came monthly to consult on the tower clock.

"The infirmarian," Kit said. "Ansa. There's something," and he stopped, because he did not have the word, and found it, and it frightened him. "There's something familiar."

Wren was quiet for a moment, looking down the yard, and when he spoke it was in the voice with the grain in it, the tale voice, pitched for two.

"Follow the burnt-out nurses," he said. "And at the center of them, burning steadily. I told you that one long ago, in another kitchen, and you wrote it down." He turned his mug the quarter turn. "Later, Kit. Her turn comes later, and it will be the hardest, and you are not ready, and neither, God knows, am I. Mend her trolleys. Learn her ward. Say nothing." And he would give nothing further that day, freight being freight.

*

He knelt for the first time at the evening office of his second week, because the Rule required it and he had signed the form, and he had braced all day for the humiliation of it, the surrender, the flag coming down.

Nothing happened.

That was the finding, and it was the strangest finding of the young ledger: he went down with the rest onto the worn stone, in the gold-brown dark with the candles standing in it, in the building that had made him cry before he could argue, and the roof did not fall, and no one collected his surrender, because no one was watching, that was the discovery, no one at all, the keepers' eyes were shut or elsewhere, the whole nave faced away from him toward the screen, and he knelt in the middle of the general kneeling as unobserved as a stone in a beach, and his knees hurt in the manner of knees, and that was all. He had asked Wren, in the workshop, months ago: tell me everything the knees are supposed to mean. And it was Brant who answered it, unasked, weeks later, passing him in the cloister after office, reading novices the way he read everything.

"You're waiting for the knees to mean something," Brant said, not stopping, dry as rope. "They don't. Meaning is above your grade, novice, and above mine. The knees are the body agreeing to be smaller than something for a quarter of an hour. What that agreement does to a given man, over years," and he was gone around the pillar, an old voice trailing in the cold, "is between him and the floor."

That night Kit lay in the novices' dormitory, in the plain hard bed among the breathing of strangers, with the dome somewhere above him holding its dark, and noticed, in the drowse before sleep, without yet noticing that he noticed, that his hands, folded on his chest in the house style, palm over palm, the way the offices had folded them five times that day, were quiet. Not gripping. Not counting. Folded, and holding, as it seemed, nothing, or else, and this thought arrived from a shelf far below the doctrine and did not wake him, holding something that had finally, after all the empty years, been put into them.
