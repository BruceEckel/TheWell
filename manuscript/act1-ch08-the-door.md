# Chapter 8: The Door

The warmth stayed longest. That would have interested him: the textbooks say hearing, hearing is the last to go, and hearing did stay late, the trolley wheel, the third-turn squeak, growing simpler each pass, becoming just an interval, then just time itself, ticking somewhere down a corridor of which nothing else remained. But under the hearing, and outlasting it, there was the warmth of two hands around one hand, and when everything else had handed in its keys, that stayed, without an owner, like a stove still warm in a house already sold.

Then that too, and what was left went down.

Down was not a direction. It was an agreement, old and unexamined, the way water and the hill have an agreement, and what had been Corin kept it without knowing it was keeping anything, and went down, and the dark it went through was warm. That is the part nobody tells: warm, and close, and shouldering gently in around whatever passes, like blankets pulled up by someone else after you are asleep.

Partway down there is a landing.

The stairs pause there; the dark opens out; and on the landing there is a door. It stands where it has always stood, in the wall that is always there, and it was open. It is always open. There was light on the other side of it, not bright, the light of a room in use, a lamp burning for someone expected, and the light lay across the landing floor in a long quiet stripe, and at the edge of the stripe, where the light ended and the stair went on, there was a join, a seam, a thin bright strip like brass let into the boards.

He went by it.

Not past it the way a man passes a thing he has declined. Past it the way a man crosses his own dark hall at night on an errand, sure of the walls, needing no lamp, a man for whom the doorway on the landing is simply not among the facts of the house. To stop would have required a stopper; to see, someone standing where seeing is done; and what went down the stair that night was no one in particular, was a direction with a little warmth still on it, was an emptied hand. All his life he had stood in doorways. On brass strips, on aluminum sills, in the recesses of dead shops, feet finding the join without being asked, keeping, out of a fidelity he never named and never billed, some appointment, some post, some watch. The vigil had been for this. This landing. This seam in this floor. This door standing open with the lamp lit beyond it, and the whole silent house of the world around it, and no one came. The stripe of light lay on the floor and held its shape, unwalked, and the stair went on down, and the door stayed open behind what was left of him the way doors stand open in houses where a child has gone missing: not in hope, exactly. In policy.

And yet.

Deep in the going-down, where nothing remained that could be said to remember, something kept a count. Handless, it counted the way hands count: armspan over armspan, knot by knot, a rhythm rather than a number, a habit of gripping laid down in some stuff older than memory and cheaper to keep than to lose. The dark grew warmer. The voices began, far off, half known, the way they are at the bottom of every well, and the water down there was already preparing its face, the one it shows, the one it swears is yours. The count went on. It did not know it was a count. It knew the way the laundromat strip knows the shoe, the way the groove in the Long Stair knows the foot: dumbly, faithfully, from use.

At the bottom there was pressure, and passage, and then the world arrived all at once, enormous, unfiled, blazing: cold light, huge sound, the first vast unbreathable excess of it, and he was seized and lifted, small as a fact, into competent hands. He cried, as the newly arrived do, at the size of what there was. And the first thing done to him, before he had a name, before he had a self to bill it to, unasked, unearned, uninvoiced, was that they wrapped him: warm cloth, wound close, tucked with two efficient movements and a third at the feet, and the warmth went into him without asking his opinion, and he accepted it, having as yet no apparatus of refusal, no ledger, no drawer, nothing to defend, and it was, for a while, entirely enough.

They set him down then, lulled and blinking, under the world's first ceiling.

His hands, an hour old, were closed in two small fists. Holding on. Keeping. As if somewhere in the long fall a rope had been put into them, and a count confided, and the hands, not knowing what they knew, had agreed, the way hands do, not to drop it.
