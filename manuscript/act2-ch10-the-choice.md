# Chapter 10: The Choice

He died in April, above the empty chandlery, in the hard bed he had never seen reason to replace, with the window doing its evening silver and Wren's hand around his hand, humming.

They had agreed on no last words, having minuted everything; the tune would do. They took it together, the young voice under the old one like a hand under an elbow, six notes up, four notes down, and out the floor, and somewhere in the fall of the tail, between the third note and the fourth, without any seam that Wren, listening with four hundred years of attention, could afterward locate, Dara went from humming to hummed.

The warmth stayed longest. That much was the same; the warmth is always the same; two hands around one hand, outlasting their owner like a stove in a sold house. Then that too, and what was left went down.

Down was still an agreement, old, unexamined, water and the hill. But there is a difference, at the very bottom of everything, between falling and descending, and the difference is the feet, and this time, all the way down through the warm shouldering dark, there were feet, and they were his.

Partway down there is a landing.

The stairs pause there; the dark opens out; and on the landing there is a door. It stands where it has always stood, in the wall that is always there, and it was open. It is always open. The light beyond it lay across the landing floor in its long quiet stripe, the light of a room in use, a lamp kept burning for someone expected, and at the stripe's edge, where the light gave out and the stair went on, the floor held its thin bright seam, like brass let into the boards.

And this time, on the stair, there was someone to see it.

He stopped. Whatever stops. He put his feet on the seam, both of them, exactly, the way feet had been finding joins in floors for a hundred years of his lives without ever being told why, and the whole long apprenticeship arrived at its trade at last: the chapel strip, the laundromat sill, the doorframes of every room that ever held a person he could not go to. All of it had been for this. He stood in the one true threshold there is, in the thin country between the light and the stair, and kept, knowingly, for the first time, the vigil.

The room beyond was in use. A lamp; a chair that knew him; a quality of quiet he had no memory of and recognized anyway, the way the hands had recognized the count: rest. Actual rest, the kind he had never once taken, the kind he had stepped sideways from at gravesides and worked through at kitchen tables and stood upright inside of at the quiet hour, cataloguing exits. It was all there, an arm's length away, lit and warm and waiting, and it had, in some way that was not a shape and was not a face and was both, his own face. Nobody would call it desertion. The one who would know had said so himself, and had meant it, and was at this moment sitting in a darkening room above a chandlery holding what he held, humming alone.

It looked like everything he had ever refused.

And he was tired. Four centuries tired; the landing knows no lies. He stood on the seam with the light on his feet, and the stair falling away below into the warm dark where the voices were, the half-known voices, the seven billion pieces, every one of them a hurt with a body, every one of them somebody's Sandra, and what went through what was left of him was not the block capitals, though they were there, holding, the way keel holds a boat: down, same as everyone, until the board is clear. What went through him, last, in no words, in the voice that comes with no radio, was smaller than that, and older than that, and it was: the corridor is dim, and the doorbell works, and somebody has to answer it.

He turned from the light.

He went down. Not falling; descending; a man on his own dark stairs, sure of the walls, needing no lamp, and his hands, which were not hands, counted as he went, knot over knot, armspan over armspan, on purpose, all the way down to the warm, and the door on the landing stayed open behind him the way doors stand open in houses where someone is expected back: not in hope, exactly. In certainty.

At the bottom there was pressure, and passage, and then the world arrived all at once, enormous, unfiled, blazing, and before the light had finished being invented, before the first cry had fully left him, the world declared where in itself he was, in the oldest voice it had:

Bells. Many bells. Bells above bells, a hillside of them, a whole city of them, ringing the evening hour down into the room where a child lay being wrapped, warm cloth, wound close, two movements and a third at the feet, his hour-old fists closed tight around the end of something no one could see. And in the window of the room, filling it entirely, near enough to touch, the color of a coin kept four hundred years in a pocket, the dome.
