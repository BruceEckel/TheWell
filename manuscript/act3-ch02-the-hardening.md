# Chapter 2: The Hardening

The wall went up the year he was eleven, and the first brick was laid at a funeral, which he would have appreciated, in another life, as economical.

It was the funeral of Master Corvel, the candle-maker from the lane's foot, a bakery regular for fifty years, and the whole Stair went down to the lower chapel for it, shops shuttered, Kit scrubbed and collared between Lena and his mother in the fourth row. He was eleven and had been to funerals before; funerals on the Stair were weather. But he had never before been eleven at one, which is the age at which the ears change, and when the speaker stood up over the coffin and began, Kit sat bolt upright as if his name had been called.

*The fire does not hate the ore*, the speaker said. *What burns away was never yours. Hold still in the burning, and remember what you came to do.* And then, later, in the commendation, with the candles standing in the incense light: that Master Corvel had gone now the way of all the sleeping world, and stood even now, past the last stair of himself, before an open door, where a lamp was kept burning for him, and was expected, and was known.

The door. The lamp. The stair.

Kit stood in the after-crowd outside the chapel while the adults did the slow grave talking that adults did, and the thing happening in him had no name he knew yet, though he would spend years giving it names, and what it was, was reshelving. Every tale in the deep stack of him was being pulled and reexamined and refiled with a new tag, and the new tag was theirs. The sleepers who never looked at doorways: the speaker had sleepers. The door on the landing with its lamp: the speaker had it, had the lamp, had it over a coffin with incense on it. Remember what you came to do: it was in the Gatherings, it was on the wall of the chapel in gilt letters a foot high, it had been hanging over every funeral of his short life, and Wren, in the workshop, in the lamplight, in the voice with the grain in it, had fed it to him with bread and dripping, and called it a story, and let him think it was theirs. His. A private country with two citizens.

It was not a private country. It was the state religion with the labels steamed off.

He was eleven, and he did the arithmetic of it standing in his collar in the cold, with the incense still on his clothes, and the arithmetic was flawless the way it is flawless at eleven: if the tales were the church's, then the tales were the church, and if the tales were the church, then the teller was the church's man, and everything, the tune, the game, the second pillar, the faces coming in out of the light, all of it reshelved, all of it, in one afternoon, under the heading he would keep for the rest of the life and sharpen all his days: things that get in without knocking.

He said it to Wren the following Sunday, in the workshop, with the deck already dealt.

"Your stories are from church."

Wren's hands went still over the cards. The clocks went on around him, forty small heartbeats, and Kit stood in the middle of them with his own heart going hard, watching the old man with the terrible new eyes, the eleven-year-old eyes, the ones that see the seam in everything and think the seam is the truth of it.

"The door and the lamp. It's in the commendation. It's on the wall in gilt. The speaker did the whole of it over Master Corvel, your door, your lamp, your stair, word for word." His voice was doing something he couldn't stop, climbing and cracking, the fury of the betrayed collector who finds the treasure mass-produced. "I told the door one to Pell last winter like it was ours. Like it was a secret. He must have laughed himself sick. It's not a secret, it's a," and he reached for the worst word the lane had given him, and found it, "it's a catechism. You've been doing catechism on me since before I could walk, in a story voice, so it wouldn't show, and my mother let you, and I want to know," eleven years old, fists down at his sides in a room full of ticking, "I want to know which one you are. A believer or a liar."

The stillness Wren kept then was very long, even for Wren.

And Kit, who at six had been able to see something in the old face and not read it, could at eleven read exactly one thing in it, because eleven reads everything as itself: he read guilt. It was not guilt. What it was would have needed the word the tales had been minding for him all these years, the word the funeral had just nailed to a chapel wall beyond his reach for three decades: the face of a man watching a wave he had himself, with vast care, aimed at a harbor, arrive instead upon a child. Dismay is too small a word, and grief is the wrong one. There is no right one. It is the face of the designer at the moment the design does the one thing it was built never to do, and does it punctually, and does it with bricks he baked himself.

"Neither," Wren said at last, quietly. "But you've no shelf for neither. Not yet. Not for a while."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's a receipt. I'll keep it for you." And he gathered the dealt cards back into the deck with his big careful hands, unhurried, the way you strike a set nobody will play on tonight, and Kit stood there a moment longer wanting the fight the way you want a fight at eleven, wanting to be argued with, wanting the old man to defend the country so it could go on existing even as enemy ground, and Wren did not defend it, and that, though neither of them said so, was the day the workshop closed.

*

What bloomed in the cleared ground, through the teens, was the mind.

It was, the schoolmasters said, an engineer's mind, and they said it with the peninsula's usual mix of pride and farewell, because minds like that went to the mainland and did not come back. Kit took apart what the lane brought him and put it back better: the shop's proofing timer, the neighbor's pump, at sixteen, famously, the funicular's brake governor, the same assembly Wren had once mended, redone now properly, Kit said, with a machined part instead of a mended one, and the word *mended* in his mouth by then had a spin on it that made his mother look up from the ovens.

He was not cruel to Wren. That was the worst of it, in the lane's slow judgment; cruelty they could have scolded. He was polite. He nodded in passing, going up, going down; he said thank you when the mended baskets came; at the workshop door, if an errand forced him to it, he stood outside the sill, exactly outside it, and conducted his business across the threshold like a man at a counter, and the lane, which had watched him grow in that doorway like a mark on a doorframe, understood that something had been unwritten, and minded its bread.

The pilgrim trade he came to despise with a specificity that adolescence alone does not explain. He had grown up inside its machinery, and now the machinery showed: the candle margins, the pin sellers' patter, the timed release of the incense before the great doors, which he clocked one Keeping Day with a stopwatch, eleven minutes before the procession, every day, a scent cue, he told Lena, like ringing a bell for dogs. He watched the pilgrims come up the Stair with their full-cup walk and their inward photographs and he saw a supply chain: grief in at the arch, coins out at the top, and the dome sitting on the whole of it like a lid on a pot. On the middle morning of the Keeping Days, sixteen now, pressed into the shop's roar against his will, he said it at last, over the counter, meaning it to carry: that they were selling bread to people who'd been sold a door, and at least the bread was real.

His mother did not stop working. She boxed the loaves for the lame man's daughter, took the coins, blessed the change the way the Stair blessed change, by rounding it down, and when the rush had passed she wiped her hands and looked at her son for a moment.

"We feed them," she said. "That's our end of it. What they're hungry for isn't your business, doorman." And then, because she was his mother and had been watching the wall go up brick by brick for five years with her forearms in dough: "You've got so you can see what everything's made of. That's a gift, love, I don't say it isn't. But you look at a loaf now and all you can tell me is the flour got weighed." She went back to the ovens. "There's a reason we don't sell flour."

*

The last years of it were quiet, which is how walls are, once built.

He sat the mainland exams at seventeen and took the peninsula's best result in a decade, and the lane was proud of him in the complicated way of lanes, and the leaving was set for autumn: an engineering scholarship, a room in a city with no bells in it, out. He counted the days the way he had once counted screws, openly, at the counter, and only his feet declined the program: on the Stair, all those last months, they still found the groove, dead center, every climb, though the boy above them had long since relocated to the dry edges of everything, and if he noticed the disagreement between his feet and his plans, he filed it where he filed the rest of what had no shelf.

He passed Wren four times that summer. Once at the second pillar on a festival morning, the old man at his old post with his hand on the stone, watching the faces come in out of the light, and their eyes met across the crowd, and Kit gave him the nod he gave the lane, civil, sealed, and Wren gave it back, and neither stopped. The mender was past fifty now and beginning to be old in the visible ways, and there was a thing Kit did not let himself see as he went by, which was that the man's face, watching him pass, had opened. Once in a thousand, a face opens instead. He had been told what to watch for. He was, that summer, the only person in the square not watching.

The night before he left for the mainland he could not sleep, which he put down to the packing, and he lay in his boyhood bed above the ovens with the dome black against the stars in the window, in the room where the bells had always arrived before the light, and found that his hands, on top of the blanket, were opening and closing, slowly, of themselves, gripping and releasing something that was not there, the way a man's hands do, said a voice from a shelf he no longer visited, at the bottom of a well, in the middle of a warm little life, and he lay still and let them do it, being too nearly asleep to prosecute, and in the workshop up the lane, under the forty heartbeats, an old man sat late over a notebook, and what he wrote that night, though Kit would not read it for years, was not FURTHER.

What he wrote was: *the water is getting nervous.*
