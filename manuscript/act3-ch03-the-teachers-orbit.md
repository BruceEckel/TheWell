# Chapter 3: The Teacher's Orbit

The mainland city had no bells, which was the first thing he loved about it, and the love lasted about a year.

He was good at the work, better than good; the scholarship renewed itself around him like weather; and by the spring of his first year he had assembled the full mainland kit, the room, the rigor, the three friends who argued properly, and had begun to notice, in the manner of a man noticing a draft in a well-sealed house, that something was getting in, or possibly out. He slept badly. The hands did their nighttime thing, gripping and releasing their nothing, and there was no one to hear him not mention it. The city's crowds moved like crowds, and he watched them from doorways, which he told himself was an engineer's habit, sightlines, and on the worst nights, three in the morning with the day's proofs done and re-done, he would surface from almost-sleep with the sense of having been, a moment before, somewhere with a lamp in it, expected, and lie there furious at nothing nameable, a man missing an appointment no one had made.

Into this, in the winter of his second year, came Roque.

Elian Roque, the poster said, though nobody used the first name: a guest lecture, hosted improbably by the engineering faculty, titled ATTENTION IS A MATERIAL. The hall was full, which annoyed Kit, who had come early to get a hostile seat, aisle, near the front, the seat you ask questions from. He knew the type from the internet: the post-church whisperers, the presence men, the industry that had grown up in the ruins of the pews, selling the same opium in minimalist packaging. He had his question drafted by the eighth minute. Roque was fiftyish, grey at both temples, dressed like a well-kept tool, and he taught, Kit had to concede, in the way good engineers taught, from examples toward principle: attention as a substance with properties, tensile strengths, fatigue behaviors; the mind as the one instrument every discipline shares and none maintains.

At the Q&A Kit stood and delivered it: that this was the temple's oldest product with the theology sanded off, stillness for the stalled, and that he'd grown up over a bakery on the Long Stair of Sareth watching the incense released on a timer, eleven minutes before every procession, and could recognize a scent cue at any distance, in any font.

The hall did its intake. Roque looked at him.

It was not the look of a man composing a comeback. It went on one second past that, then another, a full professional survey of the standing boy, and the hall's silence changed species, and when Roque finally spoke it was in the tone of a consult, one practitioner to another, quiet enough that the room had to work for it.

"You've been right your whole life," Roque said. "Has it fed you?"

And Kit, standing in the aisle with his beautiful drafted question spent, discovered that his mouth had nothing in it, and that a thousand people were watching the nothing, and that the man on the stage was not enjoying it, which was the disarming thing, the terrible thing. He was simply waiting, with something in his face that Kit had seen before somewhere and could not place, the patience of a man at a counter, and into the silence Roque said, gently, "Sit down, brother. We'll talk after," and the hall exhaled, and the lecture went on, and afterward, in the emptying foyer, a woman with cropped grey hair and a clipboard found Kit by description and said the words that opened the next six years of his life: *he asks if you'll come Thursday*.

*

The Practicum met in a converted print works by the canal, thirty people on good evenings, and it was, Kit would insist for years, nothing like a church, and he was right in every checkable particular, which was the design.

No candles. No liturgy. No promises about doors or lamps. Exercises, Roque called them, materials work: sitting watches of twenty minutes, attention run like a current through the body's circuits; walking work; the naming drill, in pairs, where you said only what was observably so, for ten minutes, which broke more newcomers than any sermon could. Everything was tested, logged, revised. Roque threw out practices the way engineers threw out failed parts, publicly, with the data. When something worked he said so and said why, and when he didn't know why he said that, and to a boy raised on gilt letters and scent cues, the whole apparatus rang like struck metal: honest, honest, honest.

He rose the way he rose everywhere. Within two years he was leading the newcomers' watches; within four he was one of the five or six who stayed when the room emptied, the ones Roque called, with irony fully priced in, the household. The work did work; that was the thing; Kit's sleep improved, his temper cooled, the seam-seeing eye acquired something like a shutter, employable at will. He was quicker in the labs, kinder at the counters of his life, better. Everyone said so. And the deepest gift, the one that bound him: the Practicum had a place of honor for exactly the faculty the Stair had scolded in him. The eye that saw through things was not, here, a fault to be prayed over. It was the entry requirement. *You've been right your whole life*, went the founding diagnosis, and here at last was a room that answered: *good, now aim it*, and Kit aimed it, at his own machinery and everyone else's, and won praise for every demolition, and rose, and it did not once occur to him, in six years, to ask what he was building, because the doctrine had an answer for that too, and the answer was: nothing. There is nothing to build. That was Roque's center, arrived at, down the years, from every direction, in a hundred formulations, each more beautiful than the last: that the self was not a project but a misunderstanding; that there was nothing to fix because nothing was broken; that the watching was the whole of it; that you were not being made, you were only being seen, and once you knew there was no one home to improve, the lifelong construction site could finally, blessedly, fall silent.

*You are already whole*, Roque told the household, the year Kit was twenty-four, on the last night of the autumn retreat. *Everything else is scaffolding on a finished house.*

And Kit, who had a wall where a childhood had been, and a doctrine now that said walls were neither good nor bad but only seen or unseen, heard it with tears in his eyes, and did not ask, because the question had no standing anywhere in the building: then why does the master keep the scaffolding lit all night?

There were, in those years, exactly two letters from home he kept. His mother wrote monthly, bakery news, Lena's wedding, the lane; he answered at the pace of a man paying a standing order. But twice a year, spring and autumn, a package came with no note: plums, or in autumn the small hard pears the Stair grew in pots, wrapped in a proofing cloth that had been mended, visibly, perfectly, the darn running across the weave like a scar that had healed better than the skin around it. No name. None needed. He ate the plums at three in the morning at his desk, every year, both times, and put the cloths, folded, in the drawer where he did not examine things, and if the practices had an exercise for what happened in his chest on those nights, ten minutes of saying only what was observably so, he never deployed it.

The lineage he learned by osmosis, as one does in households. Roque's teacher was a systems man named Aldous Hern; Hern's teacher, the fountainhead, was Vane, dead these fifty years, whose books the Practicum kept in print and quoted the way the lane quoted the Gatherings, and about whom Roque said, more than once, in the settling voice he used for the last thing of an evening: "Vane died the year I was born. To the month, I found out later. I have always felt, and you may file this under poetry, that he left a door open on his way out, and I walked in through it." The household smiled at the poetry. Kit smiled. Somewhere at the back of the smile, in a shelved country he no longer visited, the searcher from the tale, who had lost somebody in the biggest crowd there ever was, said: *stop looking at faces. Look at hands.* And Kit did not look at anyone's hands, having outgrown the tale, and so did not see what there was to see, though it sat in the household's own treasured archive for anyone who still had the tale to hand: that Vane, in the old recordings, at his signing tables, had given each stranger in turn one second of actual seeing, a second you could time; and that Roque gave the same second, untaught, to hecklers in lecture halls; and that a second is a small thing to survive a death. But it had. Twice now.

*

The retreat center sat on a headland two hours north, a decommissioned coastguard station the Practicum leased each autumn, and it was there, the year he was twenty-five, that Kit saw behind the teacher, and filed what he saw on the wrong shelf, which is to say the finest shelf he had.

It was the third night. The household kept late watch in the old signal room, then dispersed; Kit came back up near three for a forgotten jumper, climbing quietly out of habit, and found the signal room not empty. Roque stood at the seaward window with his back to the door. No lamp. The sea did its long black work below, and the teacher stood in the starlight with his hands loose at his sides, utterly still, and Kit, in the doorway, in his stockinged feet, beheld what he took to be the thing itself: the finished house, empty of scaffolding, a man at a window with no one home. It was the stillest he had ever seen a human being stand. It looked like the diagrams. It looked like arrival, like what the watches were watches toward, and Kit stood in the doorway a long minute, warming his ambition at it, and then withdrew, silent, spare jumper forgotten after all, and it was not until decades later, in another building above another water, that he would understand what he had actually seen, and that there is a stillness of presence and a stillness of absence, and that they are indistinguishable from a doorway, and that the man at the window had not been standing in the finished house. He had been standing at the top of a well with no rope, listening, the way the listening ones do, for something that had stopped.

The last morning, packing out, Roque fell in beside him on the shingle path and walked a while, and said, in the ordinary voice, "You take the doorways. Have done since you arrived. Most people want the center of the room; you post yourself at the threshold, every time, like a man on duty." The teacher looked out at the sea, easy, reading nothing into the habit, blessing it the way he blessed whatever he noticed. "Keep that. The threshold is the only honest seat in the house; you see both rooms, and belong to neither, and belonging is mostly noise anyway."

And Kit, twenty-five, walked down the shingle with the sea on his left and the blessing settling over the oldest thing in him like a patent issued for a wound, and felt what he had not felt since he was eleven, since the incense and the reshelving, since the workshop went quiet: at home. Placed. Understood at last in his exact grain by a man with no gilt on him.

There was no practice for the hands. They still woke him some nights, gripping their nothing, counting their nothing. He did not mention them to anyone, in six years, not once; some instrument in him, older than the doctrine, kept them off the syllabus; and on the worst nights he lay in the retreat-house dark with the sea working below and his hands working their invisible rope, and over the sound of both, sourceless, six notes up and four notes down, the tune would arrive from somewhere down the corridors of him, and he would let it run, telling himself it was memory, which was true, and that memory was only weather, which was the doctrine, and the tune would finish itself, tail and all, out the floor, and leave behind it, every time, like tide-wrack, one sentence in a voice with a grain in it: *the head goes under, but the hands come up still gripping.*

He slept then, usually. The doctrine had no explanation for why.
