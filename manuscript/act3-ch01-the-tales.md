# Chapter 1: The Tales

Kit Aldan was born above a bakery halfway up the Long Stair, into a house that smelled of proofing dough and coal and hot stone, in a room where the bells arrived before the light did, and he grew up believing, the way children believe the facts of their houses, that everywhere in the world the day began with bells and flour and the sound of pilgrims' feet finding the groove outside the window.

The bakery was his mother's, fourth generation, her ovens and her forearms and her rule that no one left the shop hungry on a Keeping Day whether they could pay or not. The ledgers were his father's, a quiet man who had married up the Stair, as the saying went, and who did the accounts at the till in the afternoon lull with the door propped open, so that the shop's warm air went out to the climbers and the sea air came in to the bread. Kit had a sister, Lena, older by six years, who could carry four loaves and a grudge without dropping any of them, and he had, in the way of houses on the Stair, a whole weather system of regulars, and among the regulars, prior to all memory, established before Kit had eyes to establish him with, there was Wren.

Wren was the mender at the top of the lane. He mended what came: chairs, kettles, clocks, nets in the winter when the Lorne boats tied up, the bakery's proofing baskets, once famously the funicular's brake gear while the engineer was still reading the manual. He was somewhere past thirty that year, wind-colored hair going early to grey at one temple, and he had been, Kit's mother said, simply there one spring, renting the room above the old bindery, fixing things, easy in himself, and some people, she said, come with the house, and Wren had come with the house, and that was the whole of what anyone in the lane required of the matter.

To Kit he was as fixed as the dome. Uncle Wren, though everyone knew he wasn't; the man whose workshop you could sit in on wet days among the clock parts and the wood shavings; the man who did not talk to children in the voice adults used for children, that pitched-up counterfeit, but in his own voice, level and unhurried, as if a child were simply a person who had arrived recently, which, he said once, mending a kettle, was no more than the truth.

The first thing anyone remembered about Kit himself was that the boy climbed.

Before he could properly walk he was found on the third step above the shop door, having gone up, always up, and the finding of Kit became a fixture of the lane's day, neighbors returning him like misdelivered post. It's the boy, going up again. He went up with a method, his mother said, not wandering: he went up the center of the Stair, in the groove, small feet finding the worn channel the way water finds it, and when they turned him around he came home peaceably enough, and went up again the next unwatched moment, and the lane, which had seen everything in nine centuries, folded him into its inventory of ordinary marvels: the Aldan boy climbs. On the days he could not climb he stood in the shop doorway instead, on the sill, exactly on it, watching the pilgrims go past and up, and his father, glancing over from the ledgers at the small sentry in the light, took to calling him our doorman, and the name stayed in the family the way such names do.

The tales began before memory, so that Kit could never afterward say he had first heard them; they were simply among the things that had always been true, like the bells.

They happened in the workshop, mostly, on winter evenings when the shop was shut and his mother sent the children up the lane with a covered plate, and Wren worked and talked, because a mending man's hands are busy and his voice is free, and the voice he told in was not the counterfeit voice, and it was not quite his kettle-voice either. It was a third voice, lower, with a kind of grain in it, and years later Kit would be unable to hear any voice like it without his chest going strange.

"There was a man," Wren said, fitting a clock's escapement with tweezers, "who climbed down a well on purpose."

"Why," said Kit, who was six, from the stool where he was allowed to sort screws.

"Because people he loved had fallen in, and nobody sent down after them ever came back up. The village had made its peace with the losing. He hadn't. He wasn't built to. So he trained, and he made a rope of a particular kind, and he tied knots in it as he went down, a knot for every armspan, so that whatever the dark did to his head, his hands would know the count. Because the dark down there does something, Kit. It's warm. You'd think cold. It's warm, and it's full of voices you half know, and at the bottom there's water, and the water shows you a face and tells you it's yours."

"Is it his face?"

"It's a face. That's all I'll say tonight about the water." The escapement clicked home. "And down he went, knot by knot, and the warm came up around him like bathwater, and the voices started, and by the tenth knot he had forgotten the village, and by the twentieth he had forgotten the rope, and at the bottom he stepped off into the warm and the water showed him the face, and he said, that's me, and he settled, and he raised children at the bottom of a well and called it the world."

"That's a terrible story," said Lena, twelve, from the good chair, not looking up from her book, which was how she attended.

"It's the first half of a terrible story," Wren agreed. "Here's the second half. His hands never forgot. That's the whole trick of it, and nobody tells you, so I'm telling you: the head goes under, but the hands come up still gripping. And some nights, at the bottom of the well, in the middle of his warm little life, the man's hands would ache, and he wouldn't know why, and he'd stand in his doorway opening and closing them, looking at the water, and the water would get, " the tweezers paused, "nervous."

"Why doesn't he just tie the rope to something at the top," Kit said, "before he goes down. Then he doesn't need to remember. The rope remembers."

In the lamplight Wren went still, the way he sometimes went still, exactly the way his clocks went still between the tick and the tock, and he looked at Kit for a moment with the pale eyes that never counterfeited anything, and there was something in his face that a six-year-old could see and could not read, and that Lena, looking up from her book at the silence, read wrongly as sadness.

"Because there was nobody at the top he trusted to hold it," Wren said. "You've found the flaw, little doorman. You always find the flaw. It's the same flaw every time: the top end. Somebody should have fixed the top end first." And he blew the dust off the clock, and set it going, and the workshop filled up with its small recovered heartbeat, and he said, in the ordinary voice, that anyone who sorted a full tin of screws could reasonably expect bread and dripping, and the tale was over, and had gone in, the way the tales always went in, under true-shaped, to wait.

There were others. The sleepers, who lived in a town where everyone slept walking, and did their shopping and their arguing and their growing old fast asleep, and how you could tell a sleeper: they never looked at doorways. The searcher, who had lost somebody in a crowd, in the biggest crowd there ever was, and looked for them for four hundred years, and the trick the searcher learned: stop looking at faces. Faces change. Look at hands. And the door on the landing, told only twice in all those winters, both times very quietly, with the lamp turned down: how in every house, at the turn of every stair, if you go slowly enough, there's a door that the hurrying can't see, standing open, with a light on beyond it, kept burning for someone expected; and how the whole art, the only art there is, said Wren, is to arrive at that landing awake.

And under the tales, threaded through the years of them, the smaller curriculum, which Kit would not recognize as one until he was grown: the tune. Wren hummed at the bench, six notes up, four down, and a little fall at the end that went out the floor, and Kit's mother hummed it kneading, because it was, she said, the exact length of a proper knead, six up as you fold, four down as you turn, and the tail to rest the dough, and Kit kneaded to it from the age of seven, standing on the flour bin to reach, so that the air went into his arms as much as his ears, which was, though nobody said so, the point. And the game. Spoil, on the workshop table, on winter Sundays, the deck so old the corners had gone round, and Wren teaching him the holds and the feints without appearing to teach, and losing to him, at last, when Kit was nine, without appearing to lose, and saying only, gathering the cards with his big careful hands: there. Your hands know the whole of it now. Whatever else happens, that's banked.

The year Kit was eight, on the middle morning of the Keeping Days, Wren took him up to the square to see the procession, the bakery being at full roar and glad of one less body underfoot.

The square was a sea. Pilgrims from every port, the funicular disgorging, the great doors open and breathing incense into the morning like a sleeping animal, and Wren steered them, without seeming to steer, to a place by the colonnade where the crowd thinned, beside the second pillar, and stood there with his hand on the stone the way a man stands at a ship's rail he knows.

"You always stand here," Kit said. "Every time we come."

"Old post," Wren said.

"What did you watch for?"

"Faces coming in out of the light." He said it easily, watching the procession form. "It's a good spot. You can see everyone who enters, just at the moment the dark takes them. People's faces do a thing at that moment, little doorman. Most faces close, like shutters at evening. But now and then, once in a thousand, a face opens instead. And that's the one you were watching for."

"And then what?"

"And then," Wren said, "you have somewhere to deliver the post." Which was no answer, and was filed by Kit, at eight, under the large and comfortable heading of things Wren said, alongside the tales and the tune, in the deep shelving of a childhood that had room for all of it, the bells, the bread, the sleepers, the searcher, the man in the well, the door with the lamp, all of it true-shaped, all of it waiting, and none of it yet requiring him to choose between the world it described and the world that stood in the square in the incense and the light, because he was eight, and the wall was not built, and the two worlds were, for one more year, the same warm place.

That evening, back in the workshop, Kit saw Wren write in the old notebook he kept on the high shelf, a page of block capitals in a hand that didn't match his, and under it a line in Wren's own hand, and Kit, who could read now, read the last word upside down before the book closed.

FURTHER.

"What's it say?"

"It says I'm on schedule," Wren said, and put the book away, and rubbed his face with both hands the way tired men do, and then smiled at Kit around the tiredness, and dealt the cards.
