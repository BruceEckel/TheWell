# Chapter 5: The Circling

The job board went up on the back of the wardrobe door, where visitors would never see it, which in that flat meant where Wren would see it every Thursday: routes in, listed in block capitals, each with its status tag, live, stalled, dead. By the autumn the board read like a triage sheet from a bad night.

THEFT was the first tag to die, and it died in one conversation. Dara had worked it up properly, out of professional habit, the way he pre-ran routes to addresses he hoped never to be called to: Keeping Days flow, warder rotations, the case fixings, egress. He presented it to Wren as a thought experiment, and Wren heard it through with his hands folded, and then said, "Attempt eleven. More or less that plan. Better in one respect; you thought of the funicular." He turned his mug the quarter turn. "We got it out, Dara. Had it four days. I won't itemize what those four days cost, because you'd want the itemization and freight is freight, but I'll give you the balance line: everything the church loses, it buries deeper when it gets it back. That case you're planning around used to be an open shelf. Pilgrims handled the tally cords freely for two hundred years; there's a prayer for it, the handling prayer, nobody's used it since. It's behind glass because of me." He looked at his own folded hands. "Every time we force it, we teach it. The whole building is a thing that learns. So: no. We go through doors that are offered, or we wait for one that is. We don't make doors. Making doors is how the glass happens."

So it went up as DEAD, and the offered doors went up beside it, and one by one, across the year, Dara killed them all himself.

*

The scholar's door died in the Chapter House, in the office of Canon Verest, keeper of the fabric, a courteous dry man with a squint from four decades of small print who had agreed, on the strength of a well-drafted letter, to consider sponsoring an examination request: the tally cords, comparative study, provenance and use. Materials handling, under supervision, was, he allowed, not unheard of, for accredited purposes, sponsored by the appropriate officer, which he was.

For half an hour it went well. That was the trap of the church's learned men; they were learned, they were likable, the canon had opinions about rope. And then the canon, warming, quoted the Sixth Gathering on the fire and the ore, the passage's own history being his party piece: nine centuries of unbroken text, he said, the oldest continuously recited passage in the liturgy, unchanged since the founding, and Dara, who had spent the fortnight before in the diocesan library because preparation was the trade, heard himself say, pleasantly, that this was not correct. That the pre-reform text differed in four places. That the burning line was absent entirely from the two oldest witnesses, appearing first, in a different hand, in the margins of a copy dated three centuries in, and migrating inward, as margins do, until the church that recited it unchanged had in fact composed it by increments and forgotten doing so. He cited the shelf marks. He offered, from memory, the variant readings. He was right about all of it, and he watched himself be right, from a slight elevation, with a familiar sinking, the way you watch a wave you have set going as it travels down a rope toward a thing you love.

The canon's face closed like a ledger. Not at the facts; Dara understood later, replaying it on the boat, that the facts were likely known to him, were possibly his own private grief. At the register. At being corrected in his study, from memory, with relish worn as courtesy, by a paramedic with no letters after his name and something in his eyes the canon had seen before in men who came to argue with buildings.

The sponsorship letter, when it came, was a masterpiece of the genre: warm, regretful, and a door bricked so smoothly you could not say where the opening had been.

STALLED, Dara wrote, and then, honestly, after a week: DEAD. CAUSE: SELF.

*

The steward's door took longer to die, because its requirements were procedural, and procedure he could do. Lay stewards for Keeping Days were drawn from parishioners in good standing, and standing was a checkable thing, a register kept at parish level, and the parish register wanted only this: attendance, some regularity of it, at any recognized observance. The quiet hour qualified. The quiet hour was forty minutes, no sermon, no kneeling, no declarations, tea in green cups. He had audited its structure. He could sit silently in a room; he had done little else, in a sense, for thirty-five years.

He went once.

His mother heard within the day, Lorne being Lorne; she rang him with a brightness so carefully banked, so instantly and desperately casual, *hearing you looked in at Marsh Lane on Thursday*, that he stood with the phone at his ear and his eyes shut, understanding for the first time in his adult life the exact size of the hope he had been standing on all these years, its whole buried acreage, and understanding also, in the same breath, that he was about to be its weather. *It's for a project*, he said, *don't knit anything into it*, and heard her say *of course not, love*, in the voice of a woman already knitting, and the cruelty of accuracy, the old friend, sat in his chest all week like a swallowed coin.

The hour itself he endured the way he'd endure a lecture in a field he'd once been wrong about publicly. Fourteen people, Marsh Lane, rain on the skylight. A retired ferryman read two paragraphs of the Ninth Gathering in the voice men use for other men's poetry. Silence, then, thirty minutes of it, and Dara sat upright in it with his hands on his knees, cataloguing exits, and the silence did what silence did: it pooled, it rose, it leaned its old weight on the one door, and he held the door, being practiced, and the hour ended, and a woman his mother's age put a green cup in front of him without asking, and he drank it while it was hot, and left before the second cup, and logged the visit that night in one line: *attended. no data.*

And did not go back.

That was the whole of it; that was the death of the steward's door, not in a scene but in a series of Thursdays on which he was, technically, on shift, or tired, or behind on the board, and the register at Marsh Lane acquired one entry and then acquired no more, and standing, it turned out, was not a stamp but a pulse. You could not bank it, it had to beat. In the spring a steward's form arrived for the parish's recommendation and came back with the box unticked and a kind note suggesting he was very welcome at Marsh Lane at any time, which was true, which was the worst of it, they meant it. The door stood open the way that building's doors always stood open, and he would not walk through it, and he stood in his kitchen holding the unticked box and heard himself say, aloud, alone, "I'm not going to pretend to be one of them," and heard how it sounded, and wrote DEAD on the board, and did not fill in the cause line.

Wren filled it in the following Thursday, in pencil, in his careful old hand, while Dara was making the tea. He didn't announce it. Dara found it after he'd gone.

CAUSE: TOLL UNPAID.

And underneath, smaller: *The toll isn't belief. Nobody checks belief; they can't; it's the one thing the building never asks you to prove. The toll is being seen in the queue with the needy, as one of them. You'd rather pick the lock than be seen in the queue. So would he. That's the piece I mean, when you ask which piece.*

Dara stood in front of the wardrobe door for a long time that evening. Then he closed it, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person, and did not open the board again for a month.

*

The year turned. He worked. The strait went through its winter faces. And Wren, that year, began perceptibly to be old.

It came in small drafts. The stairs took a pause at the half landing now, taken casually, disguised as an interest in the harbor. The walk to the corner acquired a stick, produced one Thursday from the canvas bag with no comment and, in the pale eyes, a dare that Dara, a professional, declined to take. The cough arrived with the fogs and stayed past them. Dara, who assessed men on stairs for a living, ran the assessment without wanting to, in the way of the trade, and got back numbers he then declined to look at, and caught himself, one Thursday, holding the teapot, listening to the breathing in the next room, counting it, and made himself stop, and could not entirely stop.

"We have a year," Wren had said, in the summer, of the plan. He had stopped saying it by the winter.

*

The Keeping opened on the fourth of June, and Dara crossed with the pilgrims.

He had told himself, and even Wren, that it was reconnaissance, and it was; he took the early boat with a deck full of cup pins and folding stools and thermoses, the walk-of-people-carrying-full-cups all around him at the rail, and he stood among them in his plain jacket, one of them to any eye, which was itself a thing he did not examine. The queue formed at the arch before eight. It went up the Long Stair at the pace of its oldest members, and he went at that pace, in the groove, hemmed in, shoulder to shoulder, no lane of his own, and found, to his complicated disgust, that the pace did something, that arriving slowly changed the arriving, and by the blind corner he had stopped rehearsing his exits.

Through the square. Through the great doors, into the gold-brown dark, which took him this time without comment, one drop in a river of drops. The nave went by like weather. And then the far screen, which he had never passed, and the low door in it, and the Keeping.

It was smaller than the word. That was the first shock: a stone room you could nearly touch both walls of, nine centuries deep, lit like dusk, lined with cases whose glass held the breath-fog of a hundred thousand pilgrims a year, and the file moved through it at the speed of the warders' quiet *keep moving, please, friends*, a pace set for looking, not for finding. He found case eleven before he meant to. Third shelf. Between a dented cup and a saint's alleged shoe: a coil of grey cord, waxed dark, knotted along its run.

Tally cord, common, period uncertain.

The file carried him toward it and would carry him past it, and he had, by his reckoning on the boat, between four and seven seconds of standing room, and what happened in them was not what he had planned, because he had planned observation. His hands came up. Both of them, unbidden, and laid themselves flat on the case glass, palms down, fingers spread, the way you lay hands on a chest to feel for the life in it, and through the cold of the glass, an inch of air and nine centuries away, the cord lay in its coil with its nine knots looking back at him, and his hands, on the glass, began, very slightly, to count. He felt them do it. Felt the fingertips find a rhythm against the pane, armspan over armspan, knot by knot, dumb, faithful, certain, the hands of a man reading a face by touch through a window, and the gooseflesh went over him from the neck down, and a warder's voice said, kindly, at his shoulder, *keep moving, please, friend*.

He kept moving. The file bore him out through the low door, through the nave, into the square's flat noon, where the pilgrims around him were weeping the way pilgrims weep, easily, sociably, handing tissues along the queue, and he stood in the light with his palms still ringing and understood the year's work in its entirety.

He had gotten close enough to look through the glass at what his own hands knew. That was the yield. Twelve months, four doors, every one of them dead and three of them killed by his own mouth, and the sum of it was thirty centimeters of air and six millimeters of glass and a warder saying friend. On the boat home he stood at the stern rail and did not laugh, not even the bark. Across the water the dome sank slowly into its thumbnail, keeping everything, patient as an institution, patient the way only things that have already won are patient, and he thought, with a flat clarity that frightened him more than rage would have: it doesn't have to do anything. It just has to be a building. I'm the one who has to change shape.

He put that thought in an envelope, so to speak, and did not date it, and did not tell Wren, and it sat where such envelopes sat, in the drawer of him, face to face with its answer, arguing quietly, for the rest of the life.
