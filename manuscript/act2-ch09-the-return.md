# Chapter 9: The Return

The knock came in November, on a Tuesday, which he would notice only afterward, at ten past four in the afternoon, with the dark already coming up the strait like a tide.

Dara was seventy-five. He had outlived the title, the car allowance, two of his own students, and the chandlery below, which was a holiday letting now that stood empty most of the year, so that he lived above a bright vacancy, the last tenant of his own life, hip permitting on good days the stairs and on bad days the view. He was doing the crossword he did not enjoy, at the table, under the window, when the knock came, and he sat still for a moment the way the old sit still, marshaling, and then went and opened the door.

A young man stood on the landing. Twenty-one, twenty-two. Work coat, wind-colored hair, a rucksack held in front of him by both straps the way men of a much earlier era held bags, and he stood with his boots exactly on the sill, on the join in the boards, and looked at Dara with pale eyes that were doing the thing eyes do at the arrivals gate, going over a face, checking it against a carried thing, finding it.

"There you are," the young man said.

The whole of Dara's life argued no. It argued with a fluency of seventy-five years' standing, it flooded the hall of him with its files and its floodlights: a student of a student, a landlady's grandson, a researcher for some anniversary piece about response times, a scammer at last come for the kettle. The arguments were superb. They were always superb. And against them stood one Tuesday, forty years old, a paper room in Tide Row and a leaf-weight hand on his wrist and a voice spending its last installments on exactly this doorway, and Dara stood in the doorway and shook, and the young man watched him shake and did not help, did not press, gave him what he had always given him, which was time, and the manner of giving it was the manner, unmistakable, four hundred years old, twenty-two years old, patient as furniture.

"Wren," Dara said.

"Mostly just Wren." The young face went sideways a degree, apologetic. "It's still true. It's the only name that comes with me."

"You're," Dara said, and his voice went and he got it back, "you're going to have to prove it. I'm sorry. I'm old, and it's been, you have no idea what it's been, and I will not be the fool at the end who wanted it so badly he—"

"The bag's on the chair under the window," Wren said. "Handles up. You never moved it. In it there's an envelope I sealed before I went, marked: for later, you'll know when." He recited it gently, eyes closed, reading an inner page. "It's one sentence. It says: the count. You'll know them by the count. And then it says: I finished the sentence forty years late. Punctuality improves."

Dara went and got the envelope. His hands did poorly with the flap, and he would not let the young man help, and the young man did not offer, knowing better, standing just inside the door with his boots together like a man in church. The paper inside was one small page, pencil, the careful old hand.

*The count. You'll know them by the count. I finished the sentence forty years late. Punctuality improves.*

Dara sat down at the table. The dark came up the strait. After a while he said, without turning around, in a voice scoured level, "There's tea in the second cupboard. You know how I take it," and heard, behind him, the young man come in at last and fill the kettle, and set out two mugs without asking where anything was, and the sound of it, the specific competence of the sound, was the sound of a language he had stopped speaking coming back into the room on its own two feet.

They took the winter to relearn each other, which surprised the young one, Dara thought, more than it surprised him.

Because it was Wren and was not. The carried thing was intact, the whole freight of him, the memory, the manner, the mission, the maddening quarter-turn of the mug; but the instrument was new, and the instrument had opinions. This Wren laughed easily, a young man's laugh that arrived before he could dress it; he ate like a docker; he took stairs two at a time and then remembered, visibly, at the turn, that the man behind him could not, and learned, over some weeks, a pace that was neither his nor Dara's but theirs. He'd grown up two lanes over, this time. Fisher family, decent, puzzled by him. He had been, he reported, a strange child, born knowing, which was, he said, its own weather to live in, you spend your first years as a spy in a small body waiting for your hands to catch up with your errand, and Dara, who had spent forty years not asking a question, asked it now, old enough at last for the answer.

"The boy on the bicycle. Blue hour, September, the seawall. Humming."

"That was the first time I found the house," Wren said. "I wasn't ready. There's no use arriving little; little can't carry the proof; you were a man who'd need the envelope, I knew that much even at eight. But I couldn't not come past." He looked into his mug. "You were at the rail a lot, that year. I used to ride the seawall until you'd gone in. I want that on the record too, since we're keeping one: some nights the humming wasn't for the going easier. It was so you'd turn around and walk to the corner. It was to get you off the rail."

The kitchen clock did its work.

"I still refuse it," Dara said, because it had to be said in the room before anything else could live there. "You should know what you've come back to. The mercy. The turns. The whole shelf of it. I'm old and I'm tired and I buried you once and I will not pretend, even now, even for you: I have not signed, and if the machine wants my signature it can come and get it. Nothing that runs on the suffering of people who never chose it gets to be called kind in my kitchen."

"I know," Wren said. "Did you think I came back for a convert?" The young face was, for a moment, entirely the old one. "I came back because it's you. That was always the whole of it, Dara. The mission's the mission; you're you; on the days those two have fought, you have never once lost." He turned the mug its quarter turn. "Besides. You have a question open. I checked, first thing, the way you check a patient's chart. Forty years and it's still open on the board: did she consent. You never wrote DEAD on that one. A man who keeps a question open for forty years isn't refusing, whatever he tells the room. He's waiting for evidence."

"And you have none."

"I have none," Wren agreed. "Where you're going, there's some. That's as far ahead as I can see, and I won't dress it up: I don't know the answer either. I've been a piece of a man for four hundred years; nobody asked me, come to that, whether I'd mind." He said it lightly, and it did not land lightly, and he let that be, the way he let things be, and outside the window the strait went about its black business, and the dome across the water held its small lit watch, and the question stayed open on the board between them, respected, alive, like a patient neither would abandon.

The ice came in January. Dara's hip took the stairs badly by then, and there was a Thursday, the fish van and the pension queue, when the lane below shone like a strait of its own, and he stood at the bottom step assessing it with a professional's eye and an old man's arithmetic, and the young man beside him bent his elbow and held it out. Not a word. An arm, offered, at the correct angle, in the correct silence, the whole art of it in the not-asking.

And Dara, who had once stepped sideways from his mother's arm at a graveside, who had let a lifetime of hands close on warm air, took it.

Took it, and walked the lane on it, arguing the entire way, some point about the fish van's parking that neither of them was listening to, his hand in the crook of the young man's elbow like a bird settled at last on a wire, and the argument was the point, the argument was the miracle of it: that a man could hold the line and hold an arm in the same hundred meters, refuse the doctrine and accept the help, unbought, uncapitulated, both. Wren said nothing about it then or ever. But that night, at the table, doing the minutes, he wrote the date in the notebook and beside it, in the old careful hand the new fingers were still growing into, one word: *further*.

*

The planning began in the spring, because Dara was eighty by the calendars that mattered, the wrist and the breath, and both of them knew what season it was.

They did it properly, at the table, with the envelopes and the notebook and the spoil deck for the breaks in which Wren beat him without appearing to hurry. JOB, Dara wrote, at the head of the clean page, in the block capitals that had steadied him for sixty years, and then looked at what his hand had written and laughed the bark once. NEXT LIFE.

"Conditions observed," he said, dictating to himself, writing. "The subject arrives with nothing. Urges, the hands, the tune. Whatever's shelved deepest. Corrections from this life's file: you found me at thirty-four and it cost us two years of proofs. Envelopes, archives, a hymnal. Waste. The window for telling a man what he is closes at about nine, Wren. I know exactly when mine closed, I was in the nave of the Cradle when it shut. Before nine, a child will believe the true thing as easily as the false. After, the wall's up, and every brick is a very good argument." He wrote: FIND EARLY. Underlined it. "Don't arrive at thirty-four with proofs. Be there at the start. Be the neighbor. Be the family friend with the bag. And tell me before I'm formed, tell me the whole of it, but not as freight." The pen stopped. He looked up, and the whole design arrived in his face at once, the way plans arrived in him, entire. "Tales, Wren. Tell it as tales. Bedtime. A man who climbs down a well on purpose. Knots in a rope. A door on a landing with a lamp. No child files a tale under false; it goes in under true-shaped, and it waits, and when the life catches up with it, the shelf's already built. You told me the whole mission in my own kitchen and I threw you out. If you'd told me at six, in the dark, in the story voice," the old voice cracked and he let it, another thing he had learned to let, "I'd have spent my whole life knowing which one I was in."

Wren wrote it down. FIND EARLY. TALES. And under it, at Dara's further dictation, the rest of the protocol: the tune, early and often, with the tail, and the words when the child could hold them. The game, spoil, for the hands. And no proofs, ever, unless asked with a notebook; the next one might be another engineer, God and the record help them all.

"And the landing," Wren said, when the page was full. He said it carefully, keeping his eyes on the notebook. "You'll come to it soon, the real one. I think you'll see it this time, Dara. I've no envelope for that; it's only the pattern: the ones who go out humming see the door. And what you do there is yours, entirely, it's the one place in the whole enterprise where nobody can be beside you. I'll say the one thing and then I'll never say it again." He looked up. "There's an exit. At the landing. For you it would open; you've earned the seeing, and the seeing is most of the fare. Through it there's the top of the well, and your own face waiting, and rest, Dara, actual rest, the kind you've never once taken. Nobody, no one, not I, would call it desertion."

The kitchen was quiet. The old man looked at the young one for a long moment, and then wrote, at the bottom of the protocol, in block capitals, the line that was to govern the next life and, though neither of them said so, the one after that, and turned the notebook around so Wren could read it.

DOWN. SAME AS EVERYONE. UNTIL THE BOARD IS CLEAR.

"Besides," Dara said, capping the pen, wry to the end of it, "you'll be down there already. Little. Humming. Somebody has to get you off the seawall."

They played spoil until the light went. And at some point in the evening, unremarked, between one hand and the next, with the strait silvering and the dome lighting its small distant watch across the water, the old man began, without noticing he had begun, six notes up and four notes down and out the floor, to hum.
