# Chapter 7: The Visitor

The unit had been rebuilt twice since his placement year, but the bones of the corridor were the same bones, and the room they gave him was room nine, and he recognized it the way you recognize a word in a language you failed to learn: not the meaning, just the shape. He had stood in this doorway once with a chart, thirty years ago, while a patient slept, having tidied the room and refilled the water, and a director had seen him at it and been unable to say what the sight had done to her. Now the bed had him in it, and other people's competence came and went around him on soft shoes, and the symmetry was not lost on him. Very little was lost on him. That had always been the trouble with dying, he thought, as a professional matter: the attention survives the uses it was trained for.

The disease was doing what it had said it would do, in the order it had said. He appreciated that about it. It was the only party to the whole business that had never once told him a comforting thing.

He ran his dying the way he had run everything, and the staff, who had seen every species of patient, catalogued him within a day and were kind about it: the ex-clinician, corner room, keeps his own chart in his head and corrects yours if you let him. Refuses the befriending volunteers, courteously, terminally. Asks after the drug chart, then has them set the driver low, lower than comfort, he was explicit about that, and the registrar pushed back once and got the whole doctrine, pain as information, the tourniquet lecture, delivered from a nest of pillows in a voice like paper but with the architecture still standing, and she wrote patient has capacity and left it, because he did, and because you cannot section a man for being consistent his whole life.

Iris had come, in the first month, down from the coast with her daughter driving. They had held a good hour. At the end she had kissed his forehead and said, "You were the smartest person I ever knew, and I watched it cost you everything, and I never once knew how to say so," and he had said, "You just did," and she had laughed the way people laugh in hospices, with the grief up under it like a floor under lino, and gone home, and they had both known the visit for what it was.

Harmon was dead two years. The last card was in the drawer at the flat with the others. Still alive, it said, in handwriting like a fallen wall. It had been wrong within the month.

So the visitor, when he came, walked into a room that had been formally closed to comfort for weeks, and the staff let him in because there was nothing on the door to say otherwise, and because he had the look, the cardigan, the slowness, the era, of exactly what they assumed he was: some last relation or colleague from the deep of the man's life, come to sit out the end.

*

He appeared on a Tuesday, in the doorway, and knocked on the frame although the door stood open.

An old man. Corin's age, near enough, or past it: big-knuckled, clean-shaven badly, in a green cardigan with the elbows gone soft, holding a canvas bag by both handles in front of him like a man arriving at a rented room. He stood in the doorway a moment longer than orientation required, feet, Corin registered, exactly on the threshold strip, and something went across the surface of Corin at that, light and fast, gone before it could be read.

"There you are," the man said.

Not a question. Not who he was looking for, not is it all right if I. There you are, in the tone of the end of a long errand, the tone of a man setting down bags inside his own front door, and he came in, put the canvas bag on the chair, moved the water glass eleven degrees around the bedtable arc to inside the reach of Corin's line-free hand, adjusted the blind two slats against the low sun in the same motion, all of it unasked, all of it correct, and sat down in the visitor's chair like sitting down again.

"I'm sorry," Corin said, in the voice for beginnings of endings. "You have the wrong room."

"I usually do," the man said. "The wrong everything, usually. I'm slow, and you move." He settled back. He had a way of settling that made the chair look like it had been his for years. "Wren," he added, as if remembering the custom.

"Wren what?"

"Mostly just Wren." He looked around the room once, unhurried, taking a kind of inventory that did not seem to be of the furniture, and nodded at what he found, and then looked at Corin with a pale-eyed candor that the catalogue in Corin, running weakly but running, marked in passing: entirely sane. Filed next to a fountain, sixty-odd years deep. "You keep the corner rooms," Wren said. "You always hold the fan of the cards. You stand in doorways when you think no one minds." He said these things mildly, factually, like a man reading a reference letter he had written himself. "And you'll want to know who I am, and the truthful answer won't fit in the visiting hours, so I'll tell you the useful part: I'm the one who's been late. Every time, so far. I have been," and here something crossed his face, the shadow of a weight, "so late, so often, that being early once will probably kill me."

"Staff," Corin said, mildly, toward the corridor. But he didn't press the button. The button lay by his hand, and his hand lay still, and he told himself it was because the man was interesting, a confabulation of unusual internal consistency, clinically worth the hour. The nurse who eventually looked in found the visitor peeling an orange in one unbroken spiral and the patient watching him do it, and she smiled the ward smile and moved on, and that was the ruling, there being no one alive with standing to appeal it.

He came every day after that. Late mornings, the good hours. He never once asked how Corin was feeling, which Corin noted with something adjacent to gratitude: the question had been put to him upward of forty times a day for a month and Wren alone seemed to know it had no answer worth the breath. Instead he brought things in the canvas bag, and set them down without asking, without ceremony, without the flicker of anyone awaiting thanks: clementines; a thermos of a lemony broth he never explained; once, absurdly, a pack of cards, and they played spoil, which Corin had taught to no one, and had not played since a woman with careful certain fingers had pointed at the card she wanted played, and he did not ask how Wren knew the game, the not-asking by then having become the whole architecture of the visits, load-bearing, agreed.

And Wren talked. That was the other thing the staff logged, fondly: he tells stories, the old fellow, lovely ones. He'd wait until the driver's small mercy took the edge of the afternoon off, and then he'd lean back with his hands folded on his stomach like a man at a fireside and start somewhere ordinary, there was a village, there was a well, and the stories all ran downhill in the same direction if you tracked them, which Corin, tracking everything to the end, did.

"There was a man," Wren said, the second week, "who climbed down a well on purpose. Sound man. Careful. The village had lost people down it, you see, over the years, good people, his own among them, and no one sent down after them ever came back up, so the ones who stayed had made their peace with the losing, which is a thing villages learn. He didn't accept it. Not built to. So he trained. He made a rope of a particular kind, and he tied knots in it as he went down, a knot for every armspan, so that whatever the dark did to him, his hands would know the count. Because the dark down there does something. It's warm, is what nobody tells you. You'd think cold. It's warm, and it's full of voices you half know, and the water at the bottom shows you a face and tells you it's yours, and you believe it, and you settle, and you raise your children at the bottom of a well and call it the world."

"Bad protocol," Corin said. His own voice came from further away in the afternoons now. "Knots in a rope. Single point of failure. He should have fixed the top end first."

"He should," Wren agreed, gravely. "He does better each time."

"Each time."

"It's a long story," Wren said. "I'm telling you the part that fits."

*

What Corin felt about the visits he did not examine, being past the era of examining things on purpose, which is one of the last mercies: the apparatus browns out before the lights do. But the feeling was there in the room like weather. It was the feeling of the seat beside you at a long meeting, filled at last, catastrophically late, by the one colleague who had also read the file. It was the platform feeling, resolved. Sixty years the rail had held its length of cold nothing and he had checked it, checked it, told no one, and here at the end of visiting hours sat an old man peeling an orange in one spiral, and the place beside Corin was, for the first time in the length of his life, occupied.

On the last Thursday he was cold, and said so.

He said so. That was all. Three words, I'm cold, spoken aloud, to another person, without arithmetic, and he heard himself say it and had no strength left to convene the meeting that would have vetoed it, and Wren didn't move for the blanket at all. Wren took his hand.

Both of his around Corin's one, dry and enormous and warm as the inside of a coat, and Corin's hand lay in them and did not pull back, there being nothing left to pull with, and nothing left that wanted to, the guard at that gate having died at his post at last, quietly, unrelieved, and the warmth went up his arm the way water goes into ground that has been dry too long, and the ground, this time, was allowed an opinion, and its opinion was yes.

"There you are," Wren said again, softly, to the hand.

The room had gone to evening. The driver ticked its small mercy. Somewhere down the corridor the tea trolley was making its rounds, wheel squeaking every third turn, the world doing its small business, faithful, uninvoiced. In the last hour the registrar came and looked at the numbers and looked at Corin and, at Corin's almost imperceptible nod, the nod of a man approving at last a motion tabled seventy years ago, adjusted the driver upward, and comfort came into him legally, officially, all invoices canceled, and he let them lower the flame, and the flame, lowering, showed the room larger than it had looked by its old light.

"Stay there," Corin said, or meant to. Something came out. Wren heard it.

"I'm not going anywhere," Wren said. "I'm very bad at it. Ask anyone." He leaned in close, then, close enough that the words arrived warm, and his old voice had no confusion in it at all, none, it was the inside voice, the appointment voice, the one that had waited by a fountain and inside a liturgy and at the bottom of every silence Corin had ever refused to permit, and he said, "Remember what you came to do. And when you get to the bottom of the well, when it's warm, and the water shows you the face, remember the knots, they're tied, I tied them myself, you'll know them by"

The tea trolley passed the door, wheel squeaking every third turn.

The trolley reached the end of the corridor, and stood, and after a while a nurse came out of room nine and made the call that is not an emergency call, the unhurried one, and in the visitor's chair the old man in the green cardigan sat on into the evening holding what he held, and no one in the building had the heart, or the standing, to tell him that visiting hours had ever ended.
