# Chapter 6: The Collapse

His mother died the year he turned sixty-three, in good order, as she would have wished: a short illness, a private room, her affairs pre-sorted into labeled folders that made the registrar smile. Corin managed everything. He was good at the mode, had been good at it since he was eight, and the family, what was left of it at the distance it was left at, said so admiringly at the reception, *he's been such a rock*, and did not notice, because the mode is designed not to be noticed, that the rock was how the man had gone missing.

At the service, Ostrine, of course, her wishes, the speaker read the fire and the ore, and Corin, sitting in the front row of a chapel with an even number of windows, found his own mouth moving along with it. All of it, word for word, *hold still in the burning*, sixty years of funerals had installed the passage in him below the level of opinion, and he stopped his mouth when he noticed, and sat with his jaw set through *remember what you came to do*, and heard it anyway, from inside, in his own bones' voice, the way you hear your name through water.

After that there was the flat, and the practice, wound down to nine patients he could not in conscience transfer, stuck ones, his kind, and the walks he took because a colleague had told him to get a dog and this was the compromise. Iris sent cards from the coast at the new year, twelve lines, warm, and he answered in eleven. Marlo he had not spoken to in a decade; Marlo kept score of things, and at some point the score had included Corin. And each spring, reliably, arriving within the same March week for nineteen years, a card from Harmon: photographs of walls, at first, the original wall, then others, the man had become in retirement a builder of dry stone walls across half the county, and then in later years just the handwriting, larger annually, the same sentence somewhere in every card like a stone reused from wall to wall: *still not comfortable, doctor, still alive.* Corin kept the cards in the office, in a folder, filed under outcomes.

That was the estate of him at sixty-six: nine patients, two correspondents, one folder of walls. A man walking a city at dusk at a pace calibrated by a cardiologist he had not yet met.

*

The infarction came in November, at ten minutes past two in the morning, and he took his own history while it was happening.

Onset: waking. Character: pressure, not pain, an elephant of the textbook kind, seated. Radiation: left jaw, left arm to the elbow. Associated symptoms: sweating, the specific dread, nausea mild. He lay in the dark cataloguing himself, and the catalogue was flawless, and it went on being flawless for what the phone would later tell him was eleven minutes, eleven, while the part of him that kept the accounts weighed the cost of being wrong at two in the morning, the paramedics on the stairs, the neighbors' doors, the fuss, the invoice of it, against the cost of being right, and the body, which had been outvoted at every meeting for sixty years, finally seized the chair, and his hand had the phone, and his voice was giving the address, and he heard himself say, at the end, absurdly, imperishably, "There's no need for the siren."

There was a stent. There was a second stent in the spring, planned. The cardiologist, a brisk kind woman who reminded him professionally of no one, told him he had been lucky, and that the eleven minutes had been very stupid, she used the actual word, which he respected, and that the muscle had taken damage they would now manage together for the rest of his life. *Together*, she said, in the plural of her trade. He heard the plural land on him and slide off, finding no purchase.

The ward did what wards do. It removed his clothes, his hours, his door, and it issued him a bed, a gown that fastened behind, and a button.

The button lay on its cord by his right hand. *Press it*, the nurses said, *if you need anything. Anything at all, that's what it's for.*

He did not press it.

The first night he needed water at three, the jug set down out of reach on the far table by a hurrying someone, an arm's length past the leads and the line in his wrist, and he lay for two hours in the dry dark doing the arithmetic of it, the nurses have a full ward, the desaturating man by the window is the real patient here, thirst is information not emergency, and in the morning the aide who brought breakfast looked at the untouched jug, at the distance between it and him, at the length of the interval, and moved it a hand's width nearer without a word, and he thanked her in the voice for concluding things.

It was not pride, he would have said. It was accounting. The button cost the presser nothing and the answerer something, and a man who had watched, at eight years old, an entire family push a white button on a dying woman's line, everyone pushing it, pushing it for her and for themselves, the button doing for that room what the fire could not, had concluded, somewhere below argument, in the bones' voice, that buttons were how you surrendered, that what came when you pressed one was comfort, and comfort was the flag coming down.

So he did not press it, and the ward, which had seen him before, many times, in many beds, worked around him the way water works around a stone.

The fourth night he was cold.

The window end of the ward held the cold the way stone holds it, and the blanket was the standard cellular blanket, and the second blanket was in the cupboard across the ward, visible, white, folded, eleven steps away for a man forbidden eleven steps, and the button lay by his hand, and he lay still under the thin blanket doing the arithmetic, which came out, as his arithmetic always came out, in favor of enduring. Endurance was at least on the ledger. He had been cold before. Cold was information. He set his jaw and watched the ward's dark ceiling and was, quietly, at quarter past four in the morning, sixty-six years old and further from every living person than he had ever been in his life, a distance he had built himself, stone by stone, and would have gone on calling it a wall if anyone had asked, and no one asked.

Footsteps came down the ward, soft ones, doing the round. An aide. Not one he had a name for; nights rotated. She was checking the window latches because of the cold, that was the errand, and on the way back along the row her step slowed at the foot of his bed, and stopped.

He kept his eyes shut. Being asleep was free.

He heard her go, and heard, thirty seconds later, the particular pneumatic sigh of the warming cabinet down the corridor, and heard her come back, and then the blanket came down over him. Out of the warmer. The weight of it first and then the warmth, tucked at the shoulder with two efficient movements and at the feet with a third, a competence of the hands so complete it required no waking of anyone, no consent, no thanks, no transaction at all, gone again already, her steps receding to the desk, done, uninvoiced, unrecorded, undeniable, and the warmth went through him the way water goes into ground that has been dry too long, without asking the ground's opinion.

Corin wept.

It arrived without stages. No welling, no thickening of the throat, none of the machinery he knew clinically and could have monitored: he went from a dry stillness to weeping the way a wall goes from standing to fallen, in one tense, and it was silent and enormous and had no content whatsoever. He was not weeping about his mother, he checked, or Elin, he checked, or the muscle, the stents, the years, he ran down the whole index while it happened and it was not about any entry on the index. It was the warmth. It was only and entirely the warmth, the unbought, unbuttoned, unbargained warmth of it, laid on him by a stranger who had wanted nothing and had already forgotten him, and some part of him that had stood at a door for sixty years with its hand on the bolt had heard the knock come at last from the outside and simply, catastrophically, opened.

*Remember what you came to do.*

It came up through the middle of the weeping, unbidden, whole, in her voice, the old woman by the fountain, the inside voice she had used, the appointment voice, forty-nine years gone and not gone anywhere, and for one moment, wide open in the dark under a stranger's warmth, he did not have a rebuttal. He did not have anything. There was only the sentence, standing in him like a figure in a doorway with the light behind it, and something in him that looked at the figure and ached, ached in the way of a limb coming back from numbness, which is to say a pain that is the feeling returning, and then the moment closed, because moments close, and he slept without noticing the border.

*

In the morning he was a cardiologist's compliant patient with salt tracks he did not mention, and by the time the consultant's round reached him he had the night written up. *Emotional lability following myocardial infarction: extensively documented, entirely expected, a known artifact of the insulted heart and the beta blockade, of no diagnostic significance, requiring no follow-up.* It was a good note. It was airtight, and he had known for a long time now what airtight was for, and he filed the night where he filed things, and the file drawer closed on it almost entirely.

Almost. He did not ask the night aide's name; asking would have made it a transaction after the fact, would have handed the warmth an invoice at last, and he found, examining the impulse not to ask, which he did permit himself to examine, briefly, on the last morning, that he wanted the blanket to stay what it had been. Unbought. The one entry in sixty years of books that had no column, and that he had, for six minutes in the dark, against all policy, not tried to give one.

They discharged him with a folder of his own now, diet sheets, titration schedules, a rehabilitation class he attended once, found to be group work with lycra, and did not attend again, substituting his own regimen, which he followed with a rigor the class could never have extracted from him. The prescribed walks lengthened through the winter. His route went where his routes had always gone, along the old streets, past the framer's that was now a bakery, and in February, without planning it, down Vellum Street, where the all-night laundromat had been a nail bar and was now nothing, whited out inside, TO LET in the window, the propped door long gone.

The doorway was still there. Doorways outlast their reasons.

He stopped. An old man in a good coat on a cold pavement, and he looked up and down the street in the way of a man establishing that he is unobserved, and then he stepped into the recess and stood on the old aluminum strip, half in and half out of a shop that no longer existed, and the strip was the same cold under his shoes, and the relief came the way it had always come, uninvoiced, from a supplier he had stopped trying to name, smaller now, or he was, but punctual, patient, faithful, the one appointment his whole scheduled life had never managed to cancel, and he stood there while the streetlight changed twice, feet on the threshold of nothing, a man keeping a vigil he would have denied keeping, over a door that went nowhere, in the only church he had.

Then he turned his collar up and finished the walk the cardiologist had prescribed, at the pace she had prescribed, and let himself into the flat, and the page, had he run it that night, would not have balanced, and he knew it, and for once, an old man now, tired, warm at the memory of warmth, he simply did not run the page.
