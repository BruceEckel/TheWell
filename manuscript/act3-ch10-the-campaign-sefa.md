# Chapter 10: The Campaign: Sefa

Ansa came back from the rest-year in October, and the order, which had spent twelve months bracing for her return the way harbors brace for a known weather system, found the system altered: the same force, the same knowledge of everyone's grandchildren, but the low note gone, the engine idling smooth, and a new habit that unsettled the young keepers more than any sternness had. She laughed now, sometimes, at nothing anyone could see, one short note, deep, like a woman remembering a joke told to her forty years ago and only now landing.

She came up to the fabric workshop her first evening back, read the board, and took a pencil to it without asking, which was her way of reporting for duty.

"The Voice first," she said. "She's at my north door; she's been at somebody's north door for nine centuries; and you've been planning her like a siege when she's a patient on my ward who's never once been seen." She looked at Wren over the glasses. "You never asked her, did you. Four hundred years of searching for Sefa, and the one creature alive who's never lost her, and you never asked."

"I asked once," Wren said. "Two hundred years back, at a fish gate in the rain. She looked straight through me and cried the cry." He turned his mug the quarter turn. "You have to understand what she is, Ansa. She's not a searcher, like me; Sefa built her for one purpose and wound her and set her going: to call the six. She was posted to call the officers. I'm crew. To her I'm weather." The pale eyes moved to Kit. "She has never in nine hundred years been answered by the thing she calls. That's the whole way in, and until the Keeping, we didn't have it."

*

They did it at dawn, at the north door, in the bread line, because there was nowhere else it could rightly be done.

The Voice, this cycle, was a woman of perhaps thirty-five, at the gate most mornings since she walked out of the hills four years ago: rope-haired, weathered, with a hand-cart webbed with string, and propped against the cart the sign, hand-lettered, gone soft at the corners, that said what the sign had said in every generation the records reached: YOU ARE NOT FROM HERE. The order fed her, per nine centuries of standing instruction. The city knew her the way you know weather. She took the bread, and blessed nobody, and cried the cry at the faces going in, morning on morning: the burn ward, the signing-in, the framed words nobody read, and, always, over and over, in the builder's voice that reached the back of everything: *remember what you came to do*.

Ansa went out to her first, with the soup, and did not set it down and move along. She set it down and sat down, on the cold step, beside the cart, a keeper of the Cradle sitting in the bread line like one of them, which stopped the whole queue for a moment, and stopped the Voice too, mid-cry, the way a clock is stopped.

"You've fed me before," the Voice said. Her speaking voice was small and rusted from disuse; the cry was the maintained instrument; the rest of her had gone to weeds around it. "Other hands. Same bread."

"Three bodies of mine," Ansa agreed. "Nine centuries of my house. And in all of it, love, we kept the one instruction, feed her, and nobody ever wrote the second, which should have been: ask her." The reading glasses came down. "So I'm asking. Not what you cry; the whole coast knows what you cry. You, yourself, the crier. What did you come to do?"

The woman looked at her for a long time. Underneath the weathering, underneath the rust, something very old moved, the way a fish moves under a boat.

"This," she said. And then, in a voice that had four hundred years of standing at gates in it, and every rain of every winter, and no self-pity, only the terrible plainness of the long-posted sentry: "Just this."

And that was when Kit came out through the north door, in his robe, with his hood back, and knelt down on the step in front of her cart, in the sight of the whole line, and answered the cry.

"I remember what I came to do," he said. Quietly. In his inside voice, the appointment voice, the one that had been waiting under all the fonts of him since a fountain in another city in another life. "Six came down. I'm the sixth. You reached me. Not this morning; sixty years ago, at a fountain, when I was fifteen and laughing at you; and before that at a funeral; and in a margin nine hundred years old, in letters leaning like people walking into wind. Every body you wore, every gate you stood, every winter of it, all of it arrived. It worked. You called and I came, and I am doing, right now, with both hands, the thing we came to do." He took a breath, and gave her the rest of it, the part that mattered, the part four centuries overdue: "You can stop now. You're heard. The watch is over, sentry. Stand down."

The bread line stood in the cold and watched a robed keeper kneel to the arch-madwoman, and what they saw happen then went into the lane's telling within the hour and was never told the same way twice. Her face, first: it opened, the shutters going up after a siege of centuries; and then her whole body, which had held the sentry's posture so long that its coming down looked structural, geological, a cliff deciding at last to be a beach. She put her hands over her face and sat there on the step, and Ansa gathered her in, cart and rope-hair and all, the way she gathered the whole world, and the Voice of nine hundred years wept into a keeper's shoulder like a girl at the end of her first week of work, and the bread line, being from the Stair, and knowing weather when it saw it, looked away and moved gently past, and let the morning have its business.

"Where is she?" Kit asked the Voice softly, later, in the infirmary kitchen, when the tea was in her and the shaking had gone. "You've always known, haven't you. The one you were made from."

The Voice looked at him over the mug she held in both hands, and something wry and exhausted crossed her face, the first joke of her new life.

"Known?" she said. "Keeper, I cry at the north door. She shouts at the arch. Two hundred meters, four hundred years. She walks past me every working day of every life, and she has never once looked at me." A pause. "You could tell her by that alone, if you knew the tales. She never looks at doorways."

*

The woman at the arch was called Vess, this cycle, and the city knew her too: fifties, cropped grey, a voice like a dropped toolbox, twenty years at the pilgrim gate with her placard (DON'T KNEEL. STAND.), working the queue like the anti-matter of the Voice: *you're feeding the thing that eats you; they sell you the burn and keep the salve; stand up, for once in your lives, stand UP.* The warders had standing instructions about her too, wearier ones: she breaks no law; leave her be. She and the church had long since worn each other into a kind of institution, the way a river and a bridge do.

Kit went to her alone, on a working morning, in his robe, which was tactics: she fell on a robe like weather on a roof.

"Keeper," she said, gravelly, delighted, squaring up. "Come to move me along? Bring more of you."

"I've come to finish a speech," Kit said.

"Whose?"

"Yours." And he stood in front of her placard in the cold morning light of the arch, with the pilgrims parting around them, and gave her back the words no one had heard since a tribunal room far up the well, spoken once by a woman with her voice worn to wire: "You have upholstered the whole world, and now you'll die of the padding, and you'll call the dying comfort, and somewhere in the softness a species that was made out of weather will forget there was ever such a thing as sky." He watched it hit. Watched the placard sag half an inch, the jaw catch halfway up, the eyes go from fight to fright to something under both. "You said that, Sefa. To the panel. The day they closed us down. And then you went and did the thing the panel forbade, with your own life for a stake, and here is the part nobody ever came to tell you, so it's four hundred years late and I'm sorry: you won."

"I don't," she said, and stopped. Her hand had gone to the placard pole like a sailor's to a stay.

"You won," Kit said again. "Look around you. Weather. Grief. Stone stairs that cost the climb. A world with sky in it, where things can still hurt enough to mean. This is the trial they refused to license, and it is running, and it has been running for four hundred years, and it works, Sefa, slowly, a knot's worth in a life, it works. You bought all of it. You stood up in the softest room in history and bought the sky over this arch with your one career, and then you came down into your own purchase, and the anger came with you, and the anger forgot what it was for. It was never against, Sefa. It was for. You have spent four hundred years picketing your own victory." He stepped closer, and said it into her face, gently, the way her part had said it to his, life after life: "Remember what you came to do."

The arch stood over them. The queue went past, parting, re-forming, nine centuries practiced at flowing around obstruction. And the woman called Vess stood with her placard, her whole face fighting itself, rage and its underside, which had been, the entire time, all the lifetimes, grief, the fighter's grief, the grief of the one who was right too early and alone too long, and she said, in a voice with the gravel suddenly gone out of it, small as the Voice's, "Nobody came. I shouted at that panel and nobody, in four hundred years, ever once came and said we heard you."

"I know," Kit said. "We're bad at it. We were always bad at it; we filed permits while you shouted; you carried the anger for six people because the other five wouldn't pick it up." He held out his hand, a workman's, flour still in the creases from the dawn shift. "We heard you. I've come to say it, and I've brought someone who's been trying to say it for nine hundred years, standing where you never look."

And he turned her by the shoulder toward the north door, two hundred meters down the wall, where the bread line moved in the cold, and where, beside the door, out of her post for the first time in living record, stood the woman with the rope hair, her cart left behind, her hands empty at her sides, looking at Sefa across the distance the two of them had kept for four centuries.

The doorway, and the woman in it. Sefa looked. For the first time in any body, at the urging of a hand on her shoulder, the shouter at the arch looked at the doorway.

What passed between the two women crossed two hundred meters of cold air without a word, and Kit, standing aside, watched it the way you watch weather arrive at last over water you have known your whole life: the placard going down, laid flat against the wall with a care that was almost ceremony; the walk, slow, then not slow; and at the end of it no embrace, not at first, just the two of them face to face by the north door, the crier and the shouter, the sent and the sender, four hundred years of parallel posts finally perpendicular. Then the Voice reached out and took Sefa's face in both weathered hands, the way you hold something you made, and said the cry once more, the last time it was ever cried in that city, softly, to its own maker, off duty at last, out the floor:

"Remember what you came to do."

"I do," said Sefa Lindqvist, in the burnt old voice, weeping the fighter's weep, upright, both fists still closed. "God help me. I do now."

*

They updated the board that night, the four of them, with the pencil going hand to hand like a toast. THE VOICE, crossed through, and beside it, in Ansa's chart-hand: *off duty; resting; ward of my house now and always*. SEFA, crossed through, and beside it, in a scrawl like a dropped toolbox, because she had demanded the pencil herself: AWAKE. FURIOUS. AVAILABLE.

Three lines remained. THE REFUSER. THE MAKER, with its one bare word, *sought*, unchanged in four hundred years. THE CHILD BY THE WINDOW.

"The hermit's mine by rights," Sefa said, reading the board like a picket roster. "I've been arguing with Havel Iyer since before your grandmothers' grandmothers. Forty years he read us the warnings, the coward, and signed last, and he's spent four centuries up a mountain refusing what he signed."

"You'll get your argument," Wren said, from the bench, where he sat smaller these evenings, husbanding, the balance lower now and all of them quietly counting it. "But arguments bounce off that man; they always did; refusal eats argument and gets stronger. It'll want something else." The pale eyes went to Kit, tired, amused, certain. "It'll want what it's always wanted, though he'd die before asking. Somebody to watch a fellow refuser lay it down, and live."
