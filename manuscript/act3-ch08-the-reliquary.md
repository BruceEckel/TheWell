# Chapter 8: The Reliquary

The glass came off the cases every year on the eve of the Keeping Days, and it came off by rota, and the rota, that year, in the ordinary course of an institution that had long since learned to use what walked in, said: fabric, Aldan.

There was no drama in it. That was the thing he would try to explain to Wren afterward and give up on: nine years of a life, three years of another, eighty of another, four hundred of the whole, and the door, when it finally opened, opened on a work order. He and the old sacrist, Keeper Marest, spent the evening of the eve in the Keeping with the brass lamps lit, working case to case with the humidity logs and the wax and the soft brushes, seal by seal, shelf by shelf, the annual conservation round that the order had performed for centuries the way his mother scaled the ovens: unceremonially, because the ceremony was tomorrow, and tonight was for the work.

Marest was called away at the second hour by the infirmary, an old keeper failing and asking for him, and he went the way keepers went to such calls, at once, pulling off his gloves, and at the low door he paused and looked back down the stone room at Kit standing among the open cases with the brush in his hand.

"Finish eleven and twelve and close up," Marest said. "The seals log is on the sill. Snuff the lamps but the end one; the end one burns tonight; it's the eve." And he was gone, and the door's old iron settled, and Kit Aldan stood alone at night in the oldest closed room in the country, with the glass off, on a work order, through every door, every single one, offered.

Case eleven stood open under the end lamp. Third shelf. Between the dented cup and the saint's alleged shoe, on velvet gone the color of dusk: the cord.

He did the honest things first, because he was who the three lives had made him: the seals log, case twelve's hygrometer, the founding relic's mount checked and waxed, Noor's relic, he knew now to whose grief he was applying beeswax, a plain box of dark wood so perfectly made that nine centuries had needed to do nothing to it but build a room, and then a temple, and then a city around it, and he ran his gloved thumb along a join his eye could not find, and said, quietly, to the box, because the room was empty and the Rule did not forbid it, "She's still here, somewhere. We haven't forgotten," which was and was not in the conservation manual.

Then he came back to case eleven, and stood before it, and took off his right glove.

The hands, to read, must be hands. Nobody had taught him that. Everybody had taught him that: a grandmother pointing at cards she could no longer hold, a mender's thick fingers on an escapement, a mother's palm flat on his back like testing bread, an infirmarian's cloth in three passes, all the hands of all the lives, the whole long faculty of them, and his own hand went out over the velvet bare, steady as a man's hand is steady when only his entire existence depends on it, and picked up the cord.

For the length of one breath it was rope. Old waxed rope, cool, heavier than it looked, the way meaning is.

Then his fingers found the first knot, and the count began, and the house of him remembered itself the way a house remembers itself when the power comes back on: all at once, in every room, from the cellars upward, every lamp his lives had ever turned off coming on together, and Kit Aldan sat down on the stone floor of the Keeping, slowly, deliberately, the way the trade had taught another of him to lower a body, with the cord in both hands now, knot by knot by knot, and remembered.

*

He remembered light with no weather in it. Rooms that were always the right temperature. A world, far up the well of years, that had finished with pain the way a project is finished: filed, costed, closed, and in the softness that remained, his whole civilization lying down. He remembered his profession, which had been the profession of all his lives because it was his to begin with: doctor of the mind, in a world where the mind's old motors had nothing left to push against. He remembered a woman, a patient, who had not left her chair in eleven years, not from weakness, from the absence of any reason sharp enough to stand up for; everything arrived; nothing cost; she sat in her perfect chair in her perfect light and wept without salt, and when he asked her what she wanted, she said, in the flat dialect of the sedated centuries, *I want to want*, and nobody in the licensing directorate would let him give her the one prescription that had ever worked on the species.

He remembered the six of them. A table, real wood, an extravagance, in the room with the racks that hummed. Amara Solis at its head because she always ended up at the head by never once sitting at it, the team's heart, who could not hear of a hurt without leaning toward it, the lean built into her skeleton. Teo Andrade with his beautiful cathedral of a mind and his terror, visible only sidelong, of any silence he had not furnished. Sefa Lindqvist, who fought the directorate in the open while the rest of them filed permits, who came to the table straight from the tribunals with her voice worn to wire and her rightness burning off her like weather off a runway. Noor Okafor, who said little and made the machine say everything, who trusted objects because objects, she said once, do not decide you are asking too much. Havel Iyer, rigorous, frightened, magnificent, who alone among them had read every warning in the literature aloud, twice, and then signed anyway, last, with the smallest handwriting on the page. And himself. Ren Ashwin. The skeptic. The one who kept demanding better controls. The one who did not trust ropes, and so tied his own.

He remembered the hearing. Kind, exhausted faces down a long table with a tremor at the end of it managed flat against the wood, and the finding read aloud in the language of care: that suffering, however administered, however consented, could not be licensed as treatment; that the applicants' protocol was, in the panel's view, a cruelty in period costume; that the machines would be sealed. He had been censured before, then. First, rather. In the original font. He remembered Sefa standing up at the back, not shouting for once, which was worse, and saying to the panel in a voice the wire had almost worn through: *you have upholstered the whole world, and now you'll die of the padding, and you'll call the dying comfort, and somewhere in the softness a species that was made out of weather will forget there was ever such a thing as sky.* And the chair of the panel saying, gently, not unkindly, with the whole tragedy already priced in: *no doubt. But we will not buy sky with pain. Not again. The meeting is closed.*

And then the far place, the sealed place, the licenses surrendered and the building officially dark, where six people had gone anyway, in secret, with machines that would keep their bodies against every schedule, because they had run the arithmetic of the alternative: a world in a chair, weeping without salt, wanting to want, forever.

He remembered the parts.

That was the room of the memory-house he had waited three lives to reenter, and it was not a horror, and this was the thing that Kit, on the floor of the Keeping, wept at first to learn: it was the opposite of a horror. The deep interviews, they had called them. Months of them. Machine-assisted, gentle, each researcher descending into their own parliament to ask, wound by wound, part by part, the question the directorate had never thought anyone would ask the suffering itself: *what do you want?* And he remembered, in his own chest, in Ren's chest, the answers rising one by one from the long-locked rooms, the child that kept accounts, the sentry on the wall, the grief that never got its turn, every part, every single one, in its own voice, saying the same thing. *Let me out. Let me try. Let me have my turn.* Not one had asked to be soothed. Not one had asked to be filed. They had asked, all of them, all the way down, for lives, and the six had listened at last where a whole civilization had spent a century plumping pillows, and had said: *then we will build you a world where turns can be taken, and we will come with you, and we will not send you anywhere we do not go ourselves.*

It was mercy. It was mercy, and it was theirs, and it had been asked for, at the root, by the very wounds that would spend it, and Kit sat on the stone with the cord in his hands and the answer to a dead paramedic's forty-year question standing open in him like a door: she consented, Dara. Before she was your mother, before she was anyone's anything, when she was one wound in one man's dark, somebody asked her, and she said *let me have my turn*. It did not close the question; nothing would ever fully close the question; the children of the parts had signed nothing, and the coin was still the coin. But it moved the question from the column marked cruelty to the column marked cost, and between those two columns, he now knew, lay the whole difference between the world being a crime and the world being a wager, and the six had made the wager with themselves as the first stake.

He remembered the five going down. In order. Amara first, of course, though the protocol said otherwise; she heard, in the machine's first full rendering of the world, a child crying, a rendered child, a test, and she was in the entry chair before the meeting to discuss it had been convened. Sefa, with her single payload, her cry, seeded ahead of her like a flare thrown down a shaft. Noor, who would not send her parts anywhere she had not inspected, and built the cord's perfect predecessor, and kissed it, which he had forgotten, which broke him now, she kissed the box, and went. Havel with his disciplines, rigid in the chair, whispering his lists. Teo last of the five, who stood the longest at the rim, who asked the machine four hundred questions on the final night, and answered, when Ren asked him at the end why he was going at all, with the sentence that was the whole of him: *because I will not be the one who understood it and stayed dry.*

And none of them came up. He remembered the months of that. The facility silent, the racks humming, five bodies in their cradles being kept, and the readouts showing lives, and further lives, the dilation doing its work, decades below to his days above, and every protocol for contact failing one by one, and the slow understanding, arriving like ice arriving on a harbor, that the well was deeper than the rope, that everyone who went down went under, and that he was going to have to design for total loss of self, and that there was exactly one thing in the building that the wash could not touch, because it was not a memory and not a mind. It was a bond.

He remembered the splitting.

The machine had rendered the part for him, at the end of the design, so that consent could be witnessed and logged, because he had insisted, his last controlled trial: and there in the interview space, patient as furniture, sat a piece of himself he barely recognized because he had used it so little, the piece that could accept comfort, the one that knew how to be helped, the hand-taker, the soup-eater, the one who could sit in a quiet hour and simply be fed, and Ren Ashwin, who had chosen it for the mission precisely because it was the piece he could most easily spare, explained the plan, the descents, the searching, the centuries, the finding and finding and finding again, and the part listened all the way through, and asked its single question.

*Will you want me back?*

And he remembered, with the cord biting his palms on the floor of the Keeping, in the two-o'clock silence of the house of the thing he had built, what he had answered, efficient, kind, already turning to the checklists, four hundred years ago, to the gentlest piece of his own soul as he sent it down a well alone:

*If this works, I won't need you.*

*

The end lamp burned. The Keeping held its nine centuries around him, and Kit Aldan sat on its floor for a long time, being also, and now knowingly, and without any collapse of one into the other, Ren Ashwin, and Corin Vale, and Dara Marek, and the boy in the blue coat, and the man in the well, holding the rope his own hands had tied on the far side of the dark.

There was no thunder in it, afterward. That was the last surprise of the long design. He had come up the Stair as a toddler in the groove; he had spent three lifetimes standing in thresholds keeping a vigil he could not name; and now the name arrived and it changed the vigil the way dawn changes a lamp: nothing extinguished, only outshone. He knew who had left the lamp on the landing, now. He had done it himself, at the end, the last hand out of the world: set the exit light, propped the door, in policy, in certainty, for whichever of them ever came up. Every open door of his lives had been his own.

He coiled the cord back onto its velvet with the care of the trade, both trades, all of them, and set it where it had lain for four hundred years, its work done, its tag facing up. *Tally cord, common, period uncertain.* He looked at the word *common* for a while. Then he closed case eleven, and logged the seal, and snuffed the lamps but the end one, which burned tonight, because it was the eve, and let himself out through the low door and the screen and the long gold-brown dark of the nave, where the candles stood, and out the great doors into the first grey of the morning of the Keeping Days.

The square was empty. The bells began above him, Tomm at his ropes in the dark of the tower, ringing the dawn down onto the pale roofs, onto the groove of the Stair, onto the whole sleeping city of the sleeping world, and across the square, by the second pillar, at his old post, stood Wren.

An old man now, truly old, come up the hill in the cold dark of the eve because he had known, of course he had known, the rota was no secret and neither, anymore, was anything else, and he stood by the pillar with his stick, watching the fabric brother come out of the doors, watching the face come out into the light. The face that opens. Once in a thousand.

Kit crossed the square. And it was his to say at last, after four hundred years of hearing it, the oldest words in the whole long story, the ones that had been carried down the well and up again, life after life after life, by the piece of him that knew how to wait, and he said them.

"There you are."
