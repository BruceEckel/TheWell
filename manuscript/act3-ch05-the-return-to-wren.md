# Chapter 5: The Return to Wren

The bakery smelled the way it had always smelled, which undid him in the doorway, which was the correct place for it.

His mother was greyer and rounder and her forearms were the same, and she looked up from the counter at her son standing in the shop door with two bags and eight years on him, and did not ask one question, then or ever: not about the teacher, whose death had reached the lane the way everything reached the lane, nor about the household, nor the burned years, nor why now. She said, "There's the end of the seed loaf and there's soup from one," and fed him at the shop table in the afternoon lull the way she fed anyone the tide washed in, and only once, passing behind him with the trays, did she rest her hand flat between his shoulder blades for the length of one breath, the way you test whether a loaf is done, and Kit, who had a doctrine's worth of names for what that touch was and what it wasn't, put his spoon down and could not pick it up again for a while.

Lena ran the ovens now, mostly. His father had died two winters back, the ledgers passing to a nephew; the lane had new children being returned like misdelivered post; the incense still went out on its timer, eleven minutes before the processions. Sareth did not do change, it did continuity with maintenance, and for three days Kit slept and ate and walked the Stair like a man relearning a language he had been fluent in before an accident.

On the fourth day he went up the lane to the workshop.

The door stood open to the spring, and the forty heartbeats were maybe thirty now, and in the middle of them Wren sat at the bench with a net across his knees, past sixty at last and gone thin at the wrists, mending. He did not look up at the shape in his doorway. He said, as if resuming, "There's tea just made."

Kit crossed the sill. Both feet. Sixteen years since he had stood inside that room, and the crossing of it took no time at all and took, by another accounting, sixteen years, and he sat down on the stool that had always been the stool, among the clocks, with his hands hanging between his knees like a man in a waiting room.

"You said once you'd keep the receipt," Kit said. "I've come for it."

Wren's hands went still on the net. Then he set it aside, and looked at Kit a long moment with the pale eyes gone paler, and what was in his face was not triumph and not relief; it was the arithmetic of a man checking a date against a schedule he has kept so long that being on it at last is almost a stranger's feeling.

"Neither," Wren said. "You asked me: a believer or a liar. The answer was neither, and you had no shelf for it. Have you got the shelf now?"

"I built it out of a drowned man," Kit said. "Yes."

"Then sit still," said Wren, "and I'll put the whole stock on it," and he got up, slow in the knees now, and took down from the high shelf the old notebook, and put it in Kit's hands, and made the tea while Kit read.

*

He read for a long time. The spring went on outside the door; the clocks kept their thirty opinions; Wren mended, and let him.

The front of the book was minutes, decades of them, in Wren's careful hand: dates, small entries, FURTHER, *the water is getting nervous*, and near the end, from this past winter: *the shelf is built. He is coming home to it.* But it was the back of the book, the oldest pages, that Kit read three times: block capitals, a strong plain hand that was no hand he knew, pressing hard enough to emboss the sheet.

JOB: WAKE FIVE. EQUIPMENT: ONE CORD, ONE OLD MAN, ONE TUNE.

FIND EARLY. *The window closes at about nine.*

TALES. *No child files a tale under false; it goes in under true-shaped, and it waits.*

*The tune, early, with the tail. The game, for the hands. No proofs unless asked with a notebook.*

DOWN. SAME AS EVERYONE. UNTIL THE BOARD IS CLEAR.

"Who wrote this," Kit said, though the room had gone strange around him, because the voice of the pages was a voice he knew from somewhere below acquaintance: the flaw-finder's voice, the block-capital certainty, the exact grain of his own mind set down in a stranger's fist.

"You did," Wren said, not pausing in his mending. "Two bodies back. An old man at a kitchen table above a chandlery, across the water, with me taking dictation where his hand shook. You were a paramedic that time. Before that a doctor of the mind, censured for saying what you've always said. You've been coming down a long time, Kit. You are the man in the well. I've told you so since you were four; I just told it true-shaped, per your own instructions, which are in your hands." He knotted off a repair. "The tales are fact. All of them. The sleepers. The searcher; you've met him; he's mending a net. The door on the landing; you've been through it more times than anyone alive, and once, two goes back, you stood on its seam and chose the dark, on purpose, to come back for the others. That's the stock, all of it, on the shelf. Take your time. I've waited four hundred years; I can wait while the tea steeps."

And the strange thing, the thing Kit would keep turning over for the rest of his life, was that it did not fall on him as revelation. It fell on him as recognition, the way the tune had fallen on Roque: a name pronounced correctly at last. The shelf was already built, had been building since before he could walk, tale by tale, knot by knot, knead by knead, and the facts came home onto it now with the small exact clicks of parts machined to fit, and what he felt, sitting on the old stool with his childhood in his hands, was not the vertigo he'd have predicted. It was the feeling from the doorways. The bag set down. The top button of the house undone.

There was one question he had to ask with his whole body, and he asked it looking at the notebook, because he could not ask it looking at Wren.

"The one who talks. Your tale had a man who described the water so well that nobody noticed he was still in it." His voice stayed almost level. "Say his name. This go. Say it."

"Elian Roque," Wren said, gently. "And before that Tomas Vane, whom your last self heard speak, and was captured by, one evening in a corn hall, the way you were captured in a lecture theatre. He catches us every time, Kit. He's the best of us and the most lost, and that's not a coincidence, it's a mechanism." He set down the net at last. "I knew where you were, those eight years. Your mother's letters, and then his events lists; I can read a syllabus. I did not come, and you're owed the why, so here it is, all three of it. Because the last time I forced truth on you before the life was ready, I lost you for sixteen years and nearly for the go. Because the crumbs are real bread, as far as they reach, and they were holding you, and a held man doesn't drown while you wait. And because, God and the record forgive me, I hoped. He'd never let one of us near him, not truly near, three lifetimes I've sat in his halls and never got past the full second. And there you were, inside the household, trusted, dear to him, humming. I hoped you'd do what I've never once managed with any of the five." The old voice, which had carried four centuries without cracking in front of Kit, cracked now. "I hoped you'd get him to the landing awake."

The clocks ticked. Kit sat very still.

"Could I have," he said. "If I'd known what he was. What I was. The night he told me about the someone on the stair, if I'd had all of this," his hand flat on the notebook, "instead of the doctrine. Could I have woken him."

"I don't know," Wren said, plainly. "Nobody has ever woken one of them; that's the whole ledger; you'd have had a better chance than anyone in four hundred years, and that is not the same as a good chance. What I know is this, and you'll take it or you won't: you did not put him in the water. He was in the water when you met him. He'd been in the water three hundred years, and you were eight years of dry land, and the last sound of that go, the last thing in his ears, was the nearest thing to his own name that anyone had offered him in three lifetimes." Wren's eyes did not let him go. "You think you hummed him under. Hear the other reading, just once, and then keep whichever you can live with: he waited until the tune was where he could reach it, and he took it with him, down, the way a man takes a light into a tunnel. He's a child now, somewhere, who won't sleep unless the window's open. Listening for the next house. Nothing is wasted, Kit. Nobody is finished. That is the only comfort I own, and I've checked it against four hundred years, and it holds."

*

They talked until the light went, and it was the old grammar, tale and objection, the flaw-finder working every joint of it, and Wren answering what he could and saying freight to what he couldn't, and somewhere past dusk the talk arrived, as it had to, at the mercy.

"I'll tell you where I still stand," Kit said, "so you're not buying a horse in the dark. I watched the best-fed mind of the age starve to death inside the doctrine that there's nothing to be hungry for. So I'm done with no ore; that lie has a body on it now. But your ore, Wren, the fire that's a craftsman, the suffering that's a turn being taken, the whole mercy: I watched him suffer. Eight years at close range. If the fire was making something with him, I never saw the piece, I only saw the burning, and no one, not you, not the notebook, not the man I apparently was, gets to tell me his despair was medicine. So here's what I've got, and it will have to be enough to hire me: they're down there. Five, four, whatever the count is this morning. Asleep in the water, like he was, waiting for a name said right, like he was. I don't need to know why the well was dug to pull people out of it. You can keep the mercy. I'll take the job."

Wren looked at him for a long time, and in the workshop dark his old face did something that had been coming, perhaps, for four hundred years: it went entirely undefended, guard and grief and patience all set down at once, the face of a man at the end of a very long shift seeing the relief crew walk in, and he reached across the bench and took the notebook, and found a pencil, and under the last entry, in the space Dara's block capitals had left, he wrote the date, and one line, and turned it around so Kit could read it.

*He took the job. Terms his own. Same terms as ever.*

"What happens now?" Kit said.

Wren got up and went to the drawer under the bench, the locked one, and brought out a photograph gone soft at the corners: a case's innards, tags and velvet, a dented cup, a saint's shoe, and centered by an old thumb, a coil of grey cord, knotted, plain as rope is plain.

"Now," Wren said, "we talk about the only door left. You've been through the arch. You've stood in the square. Your feet have kept the groove your whole life like money in the bank." He set the photograph on the notebook, gently, a man laying down the last card of a very long hand. "Case eleven. Third shelf. Thirty centimeters of air and six millimeters of glass, and every legitimate path to the far side of that glass runs through the order that keeps it. Through, Kit. Not around; two lifetimes have died on around. Robes, vows, years. The Cradle only opens to its own." The pale eyes were steady, and kind, and did not pretend it was small. "The building that made you cry before you could argue. I'm asking you to walk in and stay."

Kit looked at the photograph a long time. The cord lay in its coil, nine knots, waxed dark with the handling of hands that had, some of them, maybe, been his. Outside, down the lane, the great dome held the last of the light the way it held everything, patiently, and he heard himself answer in the only voice he had left, the one from under all the fonts, the flaw-finder, the doorman, the man in the well.

"Then I'll need to learn to kneel," he said. "You'd better tell me everything the knees are supposed to mean."
