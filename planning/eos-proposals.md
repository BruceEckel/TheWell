# act1-ch01-the-funeral.md: Elements of Style findings

Register: Act I (Corin), brittle and precise. Short declaratives, clinical diction, comfort observed from outside. Fragments and flat "X happened" sentences are the register and were not flagged. Rule 14 not applied.

## Proposals

1. **Rule 5 (do not join independent clauses by a comma).** Confidence: high.
   - Original: "The bedroom door was ajar because it was always ajar, that was policy, defended at length against his mother, who did not understand about doors."
   - Proposed: "The bedroom door was ajar because it was always ajar; that was policy, defended at length against his mother, who did not understand about doors."
   - Why: "it was always ajar" and "that was policy" are two independent clauses joined by a comma; the semicolon keeps the pace and is the author's preferred pause mark.

2. **Rule 16 (ambiguous antecedent).** Confidence: high that it is ambiguous; the fix depends on which side is meant.
   - Original: "Corin did not tell it to him. It was not evidence for his side."
   - Proposed: "Corin did not tell it to him. It was not evidence for the speaker's side." (Or "for Corin's side," if that is the sense.)
   - Why: "his" has two live candidates, the speaker (nearest, via "him") and Corin (the subject). The next sentence, "It was just Gran," reads best if the point is that the story is not evidence for anyone, but the reader has to guess which "his" to hold while getting there.

3. **Rule 16 (ambiguous antecedent).** Confidence: medium.
   - Original: "and in the quiet Corin felt a door open in the middle of him, and behind it was nothing prepared, and he knew that Corin had seen it."
   - Proposed: "and in the quiet Corin felt a door open in the middle of the speaker, and behind it was nothing prepared, and he knew that Corin had seen it."
   - Why: after "Corin felt," "him" reads for a beat as Corin's own middle. This is the chapter's central image (it returns as "the moment of the open door and nothing behind it") and should land clean the first time. The following "he" then resolves without effort.

4. **Rule 16 (ambiguous antecedent).** Confidence: medium.
   - Original: "He saw it land on her."
   - Proposed: "He saw the step land on her."
   - Why: the nearest nouns are "the arm" and "a swinging gate," so for a moment the arm or gate is what lands. The referent is the sidestep in the previous paragraph, which has no noun to bind to.

5. **Rule 16 (keep related words together) with Rule 13.** Confidence: medium.
   - Original: "Aunt Lena, one row back, said something under hers, saying it along with him, the way you sing along."
   - Proposed: "Aunt Lena, one row back, said it under her breath along with him, the way you sing along."
   - Why: "hers" reaches for "breath," which the paragraph never says (it says "breathing"), and "said something ... saying it" states the verb twice. Note: "under hers" may be a compression the author likes; it is not on the ledger's kept list, so it is flagged. If kept, the doubled "said ... saying" could still go.

6. **Rule 13 (omit needless words).** Confidence: high.

   - Proposed: "Corin sat in the back with the cold coming through the glass onto his cheek and kept the speaker's face,"
   - Why: the window and the glass are the same object; "the window's cold coming through the glass" names it twice.

7. **Rule 13 (omit needless words).** Confidence: medium.

   - Proposed: "His mother had cried in the car and then stopped crying in the parking lot, all at once, the way a tap stops."
   - Why: the second auxiliary repeats in a compound predicate; the first "had" sets the deep past and carries the clause. The author's own had-rule says the same.

8. **Rule 13 (omit needless words).** Confidence: medium.
   - Original: "It had felt like winning. It also felt, in a way Corin did not examine,"
   - Proposed: "It felt like winning. It also felt, in a way Corin did not examine,"
   - Why: the two sentences describe the same moment; the tense wobble (had felt, felt) is an accident, and the perfect earns nothing here.

9. **Rule 13 (omit needless words), with the author's rule on intensifiers on measured quantities.** Confidence: medium.
   - Original: "Corin stood beside her, one whole handspan away,"
   - Proposed: "Corin stood beside her, one handspan away,"

    - Proposed: "A strip of brass ran across the floor where the doors would meet if you closed them, and Corin's feet found the strip without being asked."
    - Why: the expletive "There was" is the one construction the brief asks to flag. Flagged as medium because the preceding fragment "Exactly there." followed by "There was" may be a deliberate echo on the word.

11. **Rule 13 (omit needless words), with the author's voice rule on distancing frames.** Confidence: medium.

   - Why: "a woman is sitting who" separates the relative from its noun; Strunk's inverted form ("Through the middle of the valley flowed a winding stream") puts "a woman" next to "who" and gives the subject the emphatic slot. The sentence's twin ("Somewhere in its records there is a tribunal's finding") keeps its shape. The fixed phrases "wants to want" and "weeps without salt" are untouched.

6. **Author's idiom screen (line-edits.md), Section V in spirit.** Confidence: medium.
   - Original: "its occupant elsewhere, far down, on the longest watch there is, by her own hand, freely."
   - Proposed: "its occupant elsewhere, far down, on the longest watch there is, under her own signature, freely."
   - Why: "by her own hand" is the standard idiom for suicide, and it sits beside a tended, warm, breathing body. The author's rule: the wrong reading, once seen, is the one the reader keeps. 9. **Voice rules (most present tense available; no stacked hypotheticals).** Confidence: medium.

10. **Rule 3 (parenthetic expressions), with the author's parentheses-for-asides rule.** Confidence: medium.

## Considered and not proposed

- "there is a sealed building, and in the sealed building there is a room": the "there is" chain is the well-tale cadence (Wren's tale register) opening the coda; Rules 10 and 13 yield to it.
- "no instruction has ever countermanded it" and "for whichever of them ever comes up": the author's rule cuts "ever" tails on absolutes but keeps them where they add scope; both set the whole four-century span.
- "has not yet fully soured": "fully" is the measurement (partly soured is what "weeks" looks like in a mug), not a hedge.
- "is not standing across the room in a green cardigan, and is not gone": Rule 11 antithesis; the two negations are the beat.
- "there being nothing that needs saying": house idiom (I.7's "there being no one alive with standing to appeal it"); Rule 13 yields.
- "marked on a certain board with one old word, kept alive as a promise": "kept alive" can attach to the word or to the woman's body, and both are true (Noor's "sought" is a promise, and the cradle is kept). Rule 16 notes the ambiguity; I left it.
- "like it is being counted somewhere. Like it appears in some column, after all. Like nothing is wasted.": Strunk's like/as objection is superseded by usage and by the woman's idiom; the passive is thematic (counted by whom is the point); ledger-quoted closure.
- "the way the tired watch, which is barely watching": decoding tail of the kept class; the watch/watching echo is the pun.
- "Sefa is already composing her opening sentence; Havel is already correcting it.": the paired "already" is the joke; the author's "cut already" rule exempts it.
- "swearing softly at her own knees": "own" earns itself (a body just re-inhabited).
- "because you have to, because everyone does, because that is the whole trade": see outside the brief.

## Outside the brief

- The ledger's coda entry (style.md, "The coda's closures") still quotes the dilation line as "They have been gone a month. They are four hundred years old." The text, after Pass 3's six-week harmonization, reads "They have been gone six weeks." The ledger side needs the update.

It sits mid-paragraph in the woman's free-indirect voice rather than as a chapter landing, so it may pass; flagging it only because it is the one thesis-shaped clause in the coda.
- "the way things were set down beside four of his lifetimes" and "through eleven bodies and four centuries": worth one check against the long-fall ruling. Corin, Dara, and Kit are three lives below; if the fourth is Ren's life above, "everyone who ever loved him" setting things down unasked sits oddly against the comfort-stuck future. If the fourth is a life in the thirty-five-year fall, the text elsewhere never shows one. Pass 3 may have settled this; I could not confirm it from the ledger.
