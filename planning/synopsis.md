# Synopsis (draft 1, 2026-08-22)

This draft embeds proposals for the two undecided mechanics (the trap and the reveal sequence) and for the ending's stance. Proposals are listed explicitly at the end. Specific scenes are illustrative placeholders, not decisions.

## Act I: The First Lifetime

The novel opens as contemporary fiction. The protagonist lives an ordinary life in our present day, shaped by urges they cannot explain: a pull toward certain strangers, a need to stand in certain places, and a reflexive contempt for religion that costs them relationships and peace. The world around them is full of struggle, and no one seems exempt from it. Fragments of something larger brush past them as superstition, relics, and street-corner prophecy, and they dismiss all of it, because that is what the contempt is for.

They make what looks like no progress at all. But once, at the end, they follow a single urge instead of refusing it: on their deathbed they let a half-mad stranger speak instead of turning away, and something in the words lodges deeper than memory. They die. The next chapter begins with a birth, and the reader now knows the rule the protagonist cannot hold onto: lifetimes cycle, and something carries.

## Act II: The Second Lifetime

The pattern is now visible to the reader: the same pulls, the same contempt, and this time a stranger who has been searching for them across lifetimes. The stranger claims, absurdly, to be a missing piece of them. This is the companion, the split-off part, carrying the mission the protagonist cannot remember. Through the companion the truth arrives in fragments: this world is a simulation; the people in it are suffering parts of real people; the protagonist came here on purpose, to wake the others.

Belief comes slowly and selectively, gated by the contempt. The simulation itself they can accept; it arrives in scientific clothing and seems to vindicate their atheism. The proof that would settle everything, the planted artifact, was absorbed centuries of internal time ago and now sits enshrined as a relic at the heart of a religion, and their contempt keeps them circling it. Then the companion speaks the deeper truth: the people here are parts, and the suffering is deliberate, it is the therapy. The protagonist recoils, because that is theodicy, the exact doctrine they have spent every lifetime despising. The refusal is what keeps their waking partial: they come to know what they are, but they reject the one truth that would complete it. They die deliberately this time, using the choice point they can now see, going again, carrying the knowledge and the companion's trust.

## Act III: The Third Lifetime

The accumulated intuitions are dense now, and the companion finds them early. The way to the relic leads through the religion itself, so the protagonist must enter and submit to the thing they despise. What submission actually demands is the one thing they have never allowed themselves in any lifetime: to accept comfort. Inside it they discover the last truth about themselves: the contempt was never armor; it was their own unresolved issue, the refusal of comfort itself, the one piece of the rescuer that the simulation was always going to make them live through. Working through it is both the mission's requirement and their own therapy. That is the trap, and the key: no one leaves unresolved, and the researchers stayed lost because living inside generates new issues faster than it resolves them.

Touching the relic completes the recognition, and the memory it releases is where the reader finally sees the frame: the future facility, the comfort-stuck world, the splitting of the companion, the entry. Exposition arrives as recovered memory, at the moment of maximum knowledge. The waking that follows is not extraction but integration, and it cannot be otherwise: no one can be woken from outside without harm. Each researcher must wake by becoming aware within the simulation, and the protagonist's work is to create the conditions for that awareness. A researcher wakes as their scattered parts resolve, which from inside the simulation looks like enemies reconciling, obsessions releasing, and strangers finding an unaccountable peace. Rescue and mercy turn out to be the same act.

## Coda

In the future, in an isolated facility, bodies stir among the machines that kept them alive. Outside is a world that made everything comfortable and stopped moving. The researchers return to it carrying the one thing it lacks, which only suffering teaches. The final scene returns inside the simulation, still running: an ordinary person in our present day, in the middle of a hard life, feels an unearned moment of clarity. Nothing points outward; the question the book was built to plant stays available and unasked on the page.
