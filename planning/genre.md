# Genre and Comps (2026-08-22)

## What the book is

Literary fiction with a speculative premise. The science-fiction machinery (the simulation, the future facility) is the book's cosmology, not its subject: one chapter and the coda show the frame; the other thirty chapters are lives, suffering, refusal, and awakening. The sf conceit does one job: it lets a skeptical reader entertain the metaphysics without signing anything, the same out it gives Dara ("no gods, plumbing").

The author's observation: it reads less like science fiction than "spiritual fiction." The industry label for that shelf is **visionary and metaphysical fiction** (a formal BISAC/Amazon category). The Well sits in the overlap: visionary fiction written with literary fiction's manners.

## Positioning

- Lead framing when querying or describing: **literary fiction with a speculative premise**.
- Secondary category: visionary and metaphysical fiction.
- Avoid leading with "spiritual fiction": as a marketing label it carries prescriptive-inspirational baggage (sermons wearing stories). The book is protected from that failure mode by its own disciplines, worth citing when useful: the subtlety rule (the reader's question offered, never forced), the skeptic's arguments allowed to be genuinely good, religion keeping its double character (control and genuine comfort both true).

## Comps

- **The Midnight Library** (Matt Haig): speculative frame, metaphysical payload, mainstream literary audience.
- **Cloud Atlas** (David Mitchell): reincarnation structure across linked lives; literary shelving.
- **A Tale for the Time Being** (Ruth Ozeki): Buddhist-inflected literary fiction, doubled worlds.
- **Never Let Me Go / Klara and the Sun** (Kazuo Ishiguro): the model for speculative premise with literary execution; the machinery offstage, the lives onstage.
- Classic visionary shelf (context, not pitch comps): Siddhartha, The Alchemist, Jonathan Livingston Seagull.
- **"The Egg"** (Andy Weir, short story): the purest small example of the same move, reincarnation-as-system delivered through an sf frame.

## One-line answers for "what is it?"

- Short: a novel about a man who climbs down a well on purpose, and takes three lifetimes to learn why.
- Genre answer: literary fiction with a speculative premise; the simulation is the cosmology, the lives are the book.
- Thematic answer: a book about whether suffering can be the working-through of something, told so the reader is free to wonder about their own world and never told to.
